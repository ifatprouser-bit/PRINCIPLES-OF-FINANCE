NOA: One question first, and it isn't about finance. Think of somebody you've worked for whose best move wasn't your best move. Not a bad person. Just somebody whose week was judged by a different measure than yours. Take a moment and find one real person.

TOM: I've got one. A manager who would never try the better new system, because if it went wrong his name was on it, and if it went well the credit spread out across everybody.

NOA: Then you already own today's idea. The owners of a firm usually don't run it. They hire somebody to run it for them. The owners are the principal, the manager is the agent, and when those two want different things, that's the agency problem.

TOM: So it isn't about lying. It's two people with different things to lose.

NOA: Often exactly that, and it costs the owners in two different ways. Your manager and the new system is the quiet one. Nothing was stolen. Nothing was even spent. Value simply wasn't created, because the safe choice protected him and cost them.

TOM: A cost made of the thing that never happened. Nobody can put that on a receipt.

NOA: The loud one you can. Money going out on what the owners wouldn't have chosen. A department head who finds the budget for a much nicer office. And the watching itself costs money, because somebody has to be paid to check.

TOM: Here's a third, and it's smaller. A shop where the manager is on a flat salary, and the same shop where she takes a share of the money the shop makes. Same woman, same shop. Only the second one stays open the extra hour on a busy evening.

NOA: And you've just named the first tool. Change how she's paid. Partly in shares and options, so her own wealth rises and falls with the owners'. Wanting the same thing works better than being told to want it.

TOM: And if pay alone isn't enough, somebody sitting above her has to be willing to replace her.

NOA: The owners choose those people, and those people can replace her. Beyond them, large outside investors and analysts watch and push. Your class names one, CalPERS, the California state workers' fund, as this kind of corporate police.

TOM: People with enough of their own money in it to make the hard questions worth asking.

NOA: And the last one is the strongest, because nobody has to arrange it. A firm run badly enough gets cheap. Once it's cheap, an outsider can buy it and put in new managers.

TOM: So being cheap enough to buy pushes her to do better, all on its own. Nobody has to actually do it for it to work on her.

NOA: Take a breath and recall one thing. The agency problem is not bad people, it's different interests. Every tool against it does the same single job. It ties the manager's own outcome to the owners'.
