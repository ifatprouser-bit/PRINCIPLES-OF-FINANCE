Day 8 · Saturday 3 October

What the shareholder gets

A share pays you twice: a dividend in your hand, and a price that moves. Value it, and you are back to the perpetuity from day 4.

Before you start: today's words · 1:14
Five minutes: recall

Day 5 was the mixed stream. Answer both on paper before you open anything.

1. A licence pays 1,000 at the end of each year from year 4 to year 9, and nothing in any other year. The rate is 8 percent. What is n, which year does the annuity value land in, and what is the licence worth today?

2. Why can you not add 1,000 received in year 2 to 1,000 received in year 5 and call the sum 2,000?

Write your two answers down, then check.

1. Years 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 is six payments, so n = 6. n counts payments, never years since today. The first payment is at year 4, so the annuity value lands one period before it, at the end of year 3.

Hop 1: 1,000 × (1 − 1.08−6) / 0.08 = 1,000 × 4.622880 = 4,622.88 dated year 3.

Hop 2: 4,622.88 / 1.083 = 4,622.88 / 1.259712 = 3,669.79 today.

Stopping after hop 1 and writing 4,622.88 is the classic lost mark on this topic.

2. Because they are not the same money. A payment in year 2 can be invested for three more years before year 5 arrives, so it is worth more. Amounts from different dates cannot be added. Bring every amount to one date first, then add.

Guess first

You buy a share for 40. One year later the share is worth 37. During that year the company paid you a dividend of 5.

Did you make money or lose money over the year, and by how much in percent?

Guess now. No answer is shown here. This is a guess, not a test. Panel 1 answers it.

1. Holding period return: the two things a share pays you

The coffee machine: a share pays you in cash and in price · 1:36

A share (a stock) is a claim on what is left in a firm after everyone else has been paid. Day 7 drew that claim on the leftovers as a picture. Today the question is different and simpler: if you hold a share for one year, what do you earn?

Two things reach you. The first is the dividend, which is cash the company hands to shareholders during the year. The second is the price change, which is what the share is worth at the end minus what you paid at the start. That price change is called a capital gain when the price rises and a capital loss when it falls.

The holding period return puts both together and shows them as a percent of what you paid. That is the formula on your exam sheet:

r = (P1 − P0 + D1) / P0
LetterWhat it is
P0The price at the start. What you paid. This is also the number you divide by.
P1The price at the end of the period.
D1The dividend paid to you during the period.
rThe return for that one period, as a decimal. Multiply by 100 for percent.

Two details decide most of the marks here. You always divide by the starting price, not the ending one. And the dividend is added even though it never shows up in the price. If you look only at the price you are reading half the story.

The green block is the only part you can spend during the year. The purple block is only worth something when you sell. The return counts both, and measures them against the 120 you paid. The green block is drawn taller than its true size so that its number can be read.

Divide by the starting price P0, always. Add the dividend, always. A price that falls can still leave you with a positive return, as long as the dividend is bigger than the fall.

Worked example

A share is bought for 120. One year later its price is 129. During that year the company paid a dividend of 4.80 a share. What is the holding period return?
1
Name the formula and pull the inputs out of the question.
r = (P1 − P0 + D1) / P0
P0 = 120, P1 = 129, D1 = 4.80.
2
Work out the price change.
129 − 120 = 9
3
Add the dividend to get the whole gain.
9 + 4.80 = 13.80
4
Divide by the starting price.
13.80 / 120 = 0.115
The holding period return is 0.115, which is 11.5 percent.

Split it in two and the number tells you more. The dividend part is 4.80 / 120 = 0.04, which is 4 percent. The price part is 9 / 120 = 0.075, which is 7.5 percent. Together, 4 + 7.5 = 11.5 percent. Those two pieces have names: the dividend part is the dividend yield, the price part is the capital gain. Splitting is a good check, because the two pieces must add back to your answer.

Try it. A share is bought for 60. A year later the price is 57. The company paid a dividend of 4.20 during the year. What is the holding period return? Say first whether you expect it to be positive or negative.

Price change: 57 − 60 = −3. That is a capital loss.

Whole gain: −3 + 4.20 = 1.20. The dividend is larger than the fall, so the holder still comes out ahead.

Return: 1.20 / 60 = 0.02, which is 2 percent.

Split check: dividend part 4.20 / 60 = 7 percent, price part −3 / 60 = −5 percent. 7 − 5 = 2 percent. The two pieces add back, so the answer holds.

This is the case the exam likes. A falling price does not automatically mean a loss.

Try it, backwards. A share is bought for 25. It pays a dividend of 1.00 during the year, and the holding period return turns out to be 8 percent. What was the price at the end of the year?

Start from the same formula and move the pieces: P1 = P0(1 + r) − D1.

25 × 1.08 = 27.00, then 27.00 − 1.00 = 26.00.

Check by going forward: (26 − 25 + 1) / 25 = 2 / 25 = 0.08. Correct.

The trap is stopping at 27.00. That is the price you would need if the share paid no dividend at all. The dividend does part of the work, so the price has to do less.

2. No growth: the share is a perpetuity in new clothes

A dividend that never changes is the old perpetuity · 1:46

Panel 1 asked what you earned over one year. Now turn the question around. If you know what investors require, what should the share be worth today?

The answer uses one idea only. A share is a promise of future dividends. Its value today is the present value of those dividends. That method has a name in this course, the dividend discount model.

Start with the easy case. The dividend is the same amount every year and it never stops. Say it out loud: a fixed payment, at equal gaps, forever. That is the perpetuity from day 4. You already have the formula and you do not need a new one.

P0 = D / r

D is the yearly dividend and r is the return investors require. Nothing about the perpetuity changed. Only the label on the cash flow changed, from "a payment" to "a dividend".

Every bar is the same height and the row never ends. That shape is a perpetuity, whatever the cash flow is called. One division does the whole job.

Preferred stock

The silent partner: paid first, fixed amount, no vote · 1:32

A preferred share is the clearest real example of this shape. It pays a fixed dividend, set when the share is issued, and that amount does not grow. Preferred shareholders are paid their dividend before ordinary shareholders get anything, and they usually have no vote. So a preferred share is valued with exactly the formula above.

An honest note. Preferred stock is listed as a topic in the course syllabus, but the class notes in this project do not spell it out. What is written above is the standard meaning from the course book, and the way to value it is the no growth model. If your lecturer said something different in class, follow him.

If the dividend never changes, the share price is one division: the dividend divided by the required return. Do not reach for the annuity formula. An annuity stops, a perpetuity does not.

Worked example

A preferred share pays a dividend of 6.00 at the end of every year and will keep doing so with no increase. Investors in shares of this risk require 8 percent. What is the share worth today? And what happens to its value if the required return falls to 6 percent?
1
Name the shape before the formula.
Same amount, once a year, no end date, no growth. That is a plain perpetuity, so P0 = D / r.
2
Put the numbers in at 8 percent.
P0 = 6.00 / 0.08 = 75.00
3
Now the required return falls to 6 percent.
P0 = 6.00 / 0.06 = 100.00
4
Check the direction.
The dividend did not change. Investors simply became willing to accept less, so the same cash is now worth more. Price up when the required return goes down. This is the same seesaw you will meet again with bonds.
At 8 percent the share is worth 75.00. At 6 percent it is worth 100.00.

Check it the other way. Buy at 75.00 and collect 6.00 a year forever: 6.00 / 75.00 = 0.08, which is the 8 percent you required. The formula and the check are the same equation read in opposite directions.

Try it. A preferred share trades at 48.00 and pays a fixed dividend of 3.60 every year forever. What return does a buyer at that price earn?

Turn P0 = D / r around: r = D / P0.

r = 3.60 / 48.00 = 0.075, which is 7.5 percent.

Check: 48.00 × 0.075 = 3.60. Correct.

Notice what this means in the exam. Price and required return are two ways of saying one thing. Give me either and I can find the other, as long as the dividend is fixed.

3. Constant growth: a growing perpetuity, so r must beat g

Last month's rent is gone: price from next year's dividend · 1:36

Real companies rarely pay the same dividend forever. A healthy firm raises it a little each year. Assume it rises by the same percent every year and call that percent g, the growth rate. The stream is now a fixed first payment that grows at a steady rate and never stops. Day 4 named that shape: a growing perpetuity.

P0 = D1 / (r − g)

The one thing people get wrong is the top of the fraction. It is D1, the dividend one year from now, not the dividend just paid. Exam questions usually hand you the dividend just paid and call it D0. You must grow it first:

D1 = D0 × (1 + g)
The escalator: the price lives in the gap between r and g · 1:44

The bottom of the fraction carries a condition. r must be larger than g. If g were equal to r the bottom would be zero and there would be no answer. If g were larger than r the formula would return a negative price, which is not a real answer either. There is a reason behind the sums. A dividend that grows faster than the rate you discount at, forever, is worth an unlimited amount, and no model can price that.

Check r > g before you divide. Then check that the number on top is next year's dividend. Those two checks catch most of the lost marks on this formula.

Each bar is taller than the one before by the same percent. The first bar is D₁, one year from today, which is why the dividend just paid has to be grown once before it goes into the formula.

Worked example, from the class notes

In 1992, Southwestern Bell paid dividends per share of 2.73. Its earnings and dividends had grown at 6 percent a year between 1988 and 1992 and were expected to grow at the same rate in the long term. The rate of return required by investors on shares of equal risk was 12.23 percent. What is the value of this share?
1
Name the shape and check the condition.
A dividend that grows at a steady 6 percent with no end date is a growing perpetuity. r = 12.23 percent and g = 6 percent, so r is larger than g and the formula may be used.
2
The 2.73 was already paid, so grow it one year.
D1 = 2.73 × 1.06 = 2.8938
3
Work out the bottom of the fraction.
r − g = 0.1223 − 0.06 = 0.0623
4
Divide.
P0 = 2.8938 / 0.0623 = 46.45
The share is worth 46.45.

Sense check. If the dividend never grew, the share would be worth 2.73 / 0.1223 = 22.32. Growth roughly doubles it. That is the right direction and a believable size, because 6 percent growth eats most of a 12.23 percent required return.

Try it, backwards. A share trades at 30.00 today. It will pay a dividend of 1.50 at the end of the year, and the dividend is expected to grow 4 percent a year forever. What return are buyers at 30.00 requiring?

Rearrange P0 = D1 / (r − g) into r = D1 / P0 + g.

1.50 / 30.00 = 0.05, then 0.05 + 0.04 = 0.09, which is 9 percent.

Check: 1.50 / (0.09 − 0.04) = 1.50 / 0.05 = 30.00. Correct.

Read the two parts. The 5 percent is the dividend yield, the cash you get next year against the price. The 4 percent is the growth, which shows up as a rising price. Panel 1 called those the same two pieces. It is the same idea reaching you from the other end.

The 1.50 here is already next year's dividend, so it does not get grown again.

Try it. A share is expected to pay 5.00 next year. Its dividend is expected to grow 8 percent a year forever, and investors require 6 percent. What is the share worth?

Check the condition first: r = 6 percent, g = 8 percent, so r is not larger than g.

If you push on anyway you get 5.00 / (0.06 − 0.08) = 5.00 / (−0.02) = −250.00. A share cannot be worth minus 250. The negative sign is the model telling you it does not apply.

The correct answer is that the constant growth model cannot value this share. A dividend growing forever faster than the rate you discount at has no finite present value.

In real life a firm cannot grow faster than its economy forever. So this guess would be replaced by a few years of fast growth and then a slower steady rate. That extra step is beyond this course. For the exam, the point is to spot the broken condition and stop.

4. Profit, and what the manager is actually aiming at

This panel is a set of concept topics from the professor's exam guide. They sit here because they are all about the shareholder: how well the firm earns for her, and whether the manager is trying to do that at all.

ROA against ROE

Two food carts: ROA against ROE, and debt is the gap · 1:49

Two ratios measure profit, and they differ only in what you compare the profit to. ROA, return on assets, compares profit to everything the firm owns. It answers: how well do the assets work. ROE, return on equity, compares the same profit to the shareholders' own money. It answers: how well did the owners do.

ROA = net income / total assets   ·   ROE = net income / equity

The gap between them is debt, and nothing else. Assets are paid for by equity plus debt. If a firm borrows, its equity is smaller than its assets. The same profit is then divided by a smaller number, so ROE comes out higher than ROA. A firm with no debt has equity equal to assets, so its two ratios are the same number.

The rainy month: debt makes ROE swing both ways · 1:26

Borrowing is not a free gain. It lifts ROE when the firm earns money, and it drives ROE down faster than ROA when the firm loses money. That is the meaning of leverage, and it is why ROE alone never tells you whether a firm is well run.

An honest note. The class notes in this project describe ROA and ROE in words but do not print a formula for either. The two formulas above are the standard course book ones. Some books use operating profit instead of net income in ROA. If your lecturer wrote a specific version on the board, that version wins.

Same profit, but you divide by something different. ROA divides by assets, ROE divides by equity. Debt is the only reason they differ, and it makes ROE swing further in both directions.

Worked example

Firm Alpha owns assets of 500. It is financed by 300 of debt and 200 of equity. Last year its net income, after paying interest, was 40. Firm Beta owns the same 500 of assets and also earned net income of 40, but it has no debt at all. Find ROA and ROE for each firm.
1
Alpha's ROA. Divide profit by everything it owns.
40 / 500 = 0.08 = 8 percent
2
Alpha's ROE. Divide the same profit by the owners' money.
40 / 200 = 0.20 = 20 percent
3
Beta has no debt, so its equity is the whole 500.
ROA = 40 / 500 = 8 percent    ROE = 40 / 500 = 8 percent
4
Read the comparison.
The two firms use their assets equally well, and the equal ROA of 8 percent says so. Alpha's owners did better, 20 percent against 8 percent, because they put in only 200 of their own money and borrowed the rest.
Alpha: ROA 8 percent, ROE 20 percent. Beta: ROA 8 percent, ROE 8 percent.

What the financial manager is trying to do

The cafe bonus: why share value beats profit and the other goals · 1:47

The exam guide lists the possible goals of the financial manager, and the course gives one answer. These are the goals on the list, and what is wrong with each.

Possible goalThe problem with it
Maximise profitProfit in which year. A manager can raise this year's profit by cutting spending that the firm needs next year. It also ignores risk.
Minimise costsThe cheapest firm is one that does nothing. Costs are worth paying when they bring in more than they cost.
Maximise market shareShare can be bought by selling below cost. Selling more while losing money on each sale destroys value.
Survive, or avoid bankruptcySafe, and usually poor. Keeping away from every risk means turning down good projects.
The course answerMaximise the current value of the firm's shares, which is shareholder wealth. It already counts timing, risk and every future year at once.

Notice why the last line is not a separate rule. A share price is the present value of the dividends the firm will pay. Raising that price means making those future dividends bigger, safer or sooner. Everything on this page has been about the same number.

Value maximisation and sustainability

Money, environment, people: the triple bottom line · 1:24

The course raises one more point next to that goal. Sustainability means managing what the notes call the triple bottom line: the money result, the result for the environment and the result for people. It is not the same thing as pure value maximisation, and the course says so plainly. It also says the two are connected. A firm that harms its workers, its customers or its environment tends to pay for it later, in fines, in lost customers and in the price of its shares.

Carry these five lines into the exam
  • Holding period return divides by the starting price and always includes the dividend.
  • A price that falls can still give a positive return. Check the dividend before you answer.
  • No growth means P₀ = D / r, a plain perpetuity. Growth means P₀ = D₁ / (r − g).
  • D₁ is next year's dividend. If the question gives you the one just paid, multiply by (1 + g) first.
  • ROE is above ROA only because of debt, and debt makes ROE fall further in a bad year too.
