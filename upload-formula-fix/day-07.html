Day 7 · Friday 2 October

Who gets paid first

One pot of money, three claims on it. The order of payment decides everything.

Before you start: one tap, three containers, today's words · 1:12
Five minutes: recall

Day 4 was the perpetuity. Answer both on paper before you open anything.

1. A contract pays 45 at the end of every year, forever. Nothing changes and nothing stops. The required rate of return is 9 percent. What is it worth today?

2. A share pays 7 at the end of next year. After that the payment grows by 4 percent every year, forever. The required rate of return is 10 percent. What is the share worth today? And what goes wrong if the required rate of return is 4 percent instead?

Write your two answers down, then check.

1. Plain perpetuity: PV = CF / r = 45 / 0.09 = 500.00

Forever does not mean an endless value. Each later payment is worth less today, and the whole run adds up to a number you can write down.

2. Growing perpetuity: PV = CF1 / (r − g) = 7 / (0.10 − 0.04) = 7 / 0.06 = 116.67

The top of that formula is the payment at the end of year 1, not the payment made today.

If r is 4 percent, then r − g = 0. You would divide by zero. The growing perpetuity formula only works when r is bigger than g. If r is not bigger than g, the formula gives nothing usable and the right exam answer is that the value cannot be found this way.

At the date the debt matures, a firm's assets are worth 80. The firm owes 50 to its senior lender and 45 to its junior lender on that same date.

A classmate writes down the three payments: senior 50, junior 45, equity −15. Without doing any long sums, how can you tell that at least one of those three numbers is wrong?

1 · The waterfall: senior, then junior, then equity

A firm borrows money in more than one way, and the ways are not equal. This is the Merton (1974) structure your course uses. One firm, one asset, one date when all the debt is due.

The three claims

ClaimWhat it is
Senior debtMoney the firm borrowed that must be repaid first. FS is the amount promised to this lender on the maturity date, which is the face value plus any coupon due on that date.
Junior debtAlso called subordinated debt. Subordinated means it stands behind. It is paid only after the senior lender has been paid in full. FJ is the amount promised to it.
EquityThe owners, also called stockholders or shareholders. They are last. They receive what is left over, and often that is nothing.

APR means Absolute Priority Rule

In this course, and on this exam, APR stands for Absolute Priority Rule. It does not mean annual percentage rate. When a question says "assume APR holds at debt maturity", it is telling you that the order above holds. Senior in full first, then junior, then equity. Nothing else.

Read this part of any exam question slowly. Those three letters cost marks every year.

The picture that makes it stick

Stacked buckets: senior fills first, then junior, then the pool · 1:54

Picture one tap and three containers, one below the other. The senior bucket is at the top. The junior bucket sits below it. The equity pool sits at the bottom. The tap is the firm's assets, and it pours into the senior bucket first.

The junior bucket is fed only by water going over the top edge of the senior one. The pool is fed only by water going over the top edge of the junior one. A bucket that is not full passes nothing down. Each bucket has a top edge, so it holds a fixed amount. The pool has no top edge at all.

The same stack at three asset values. FS 60 and FJ 40 are drawn at 1.2 pixels for every 1 of money in all three panels, so you can compare the water levels.

The three formulas from the sheet and the class slides

VT is the value of the firm's assets on the maturity date. It is an asset value, not a cash flow, so nothing here is discounted. You are splitting one pot on one date.

WhoPayoff at maturity
Senior debtMin(VT, FS)
Junior debt0 if VT < FS, otherwise Min(VT − FS, FJ)
EquityMax(VT − FS − FJ, 0). From the class slides.

The formula sheet writes the equity payoff as Max(VT − FS, 0). That line is the right one for a firm with only one class of debt. When the firm has senior and junior debt, equity is what is left after both, which is Max(VT − FS − FJ, 0). Your class slides and the class Excel model both use the second form. The course files win, so use them. One check settles it every time: the three payoffs must add up to exactly VT.

Senior takes the smaller of what it is owed and what exists. Junior takes what is left after senior, capped at what it is owed. Equity takes what is left after both, and never less than zero.

A ceiling for the lenders, no ceiling for the owners

Lenders have a ceiling, owners have a floor · 1:52

A bucket has a top edge, so it fills and then it stops. Pour in twice as much water and the senior debt holders still take exactly what they were promised, the face value of their debt, and no more. A good year and an amazing year pay them the same amount. The junior debt holders have a top edge too.

The pool has no top edge. Everything above the two promises belongs to the shareholders, however large it gets. And the pool cannot hold less than nothing, so in a bad year the shareholders get zero and nobody can ask them for more. That is the whole difference between lending to a firm and owning one.

The lenders are capped. The owners are not, in either direction: they can receive any amount above the promises, or nothing at all.

Worked example

A firm holds one asset, worth 100 today. It is financed with 20 of equity, 40 of senior debt and 40 of junior debt. Both debts mature in one year. The senior debt pays a coupon rate of 8 percent, so on the maturity date the firm owes it 40 + 3.2 = 43.2. The junior debt pays a coupon rate of 20 percent, so the firm owes it 40 + 8 = 48. One year later the asset is worth 70. What does each of the three claim holders receive?
1
Write down the two promised amounts and add them.
FS = 43.2  ·  FJ = 48  ·  FS + FJ = 91.2
These two numbers are the only borders you need. Everything else follows from where VT sits against them.
2
Compare VT with FS.
70 > 43.2, so the senior lender is paid in full: Min(70, 43.2) = 43.2
3
Compare VT with FS + FJ.
70 < 91.2, so there is not enough for both debts. Equity gets nothing.
Equity = Max(70 − 91.2, 0) = Max(−21.2, 0) = 0
4
Give the junior lender what is left.
Junior = Min(70 − 43.2, 48) = Min(26.8, 48) = 26.8
5
Check that the three payments add back to VT.
43.2 + 26.8 + 0 = 70
Senior 43.2, junior 26.8, equity 0.

What this feels like for each holder. The senior lender paid 40 and gets 43.2 back, a return of (43.2 − 40) / 40 = +8 percent, exactly the promised coupon rate. The junior lender paid 40 and gets 26.8 back, a return of (26.8 − 40) / 40 = −33 percent. The owners paid 20 and get 0, a return of −100 percent. Same firm, same year, three very different outcomes.

Try it. Same firm, same two debts. One year later the asset is worth 120 instead. What does each holder receive, and what return do the owners earn on their 20?

120 is above 43.2, so senior gets 43.2 in full.

120 is above 91.2, so both debts are paid in full. Junior gets Min(120 − 43.2, 48) = Min(76.8, 48) = 48.

Equity = Max(120 − 91.2, 0) = 28.8.

Check: 43.2 + 48 + 28.8 = 120. Correct.

Owners' return: (28.8 − 20) / 20 = +44 percent. The asset rose 20 percent and the owners gained 44 percent. That gap is leverage, the effect of borrowed money. The debt payments are fixed, so every extra 1 of asset value goes straight to the owners.

Try it. Same firm again. This time the asset is worth only 30 at maturity. What does each holder receive?

30 is below 43.2, so the senior lender takes everything there is: Min(30, 43.2) = 30.

Junior: VT is below FS, so junior gets 0.

Equity = Max(30 − 91.2, 0) = 0.

Check: 30 + 0 + 0 = 30. Correct.

The senior lender paid 40 and receives 30, a return of (30 − 40) / 40 = −25 percent. Even the first in line can lose money. Being senior caps your loss, it does not remove it.

2 · The method: find the state, then make the numbers add up

Most exam questions on this topic give you VT, FS and FJ, then offer four sets of three payments. You do not have to value anything. You have to place VT on the line and then test the options.

Two comparisons, three states

StateWhere VT sitsSeniorJuniorEquity
IVT < FSVT00
IIFS ≤ VT < FS + FJ FSVT − FS0
IIIVT ≥ FS + FJ FSFJVT − FS − FJ

This table is from your class slides on the Merton structure.

Notice the borders. At exactly VT = FS you are in state II, and the junior lender receives VT − FS, which is zero. State I and state II meet there and give the same three numbers, so a value sitting exactly on the border is never in doubt.

The two checks that do most of the work

Adding up is not enough: equity is never below zero · 1:37

Check one: the three payments must add up to exactly VT. The firm has one pot and it is fully shared out. Nothing is created and nothing disappears. In most exam questions this single test kills two or three of the four options before you work anything out.

Check two: equity can never be negative. A corporation has limited liability, which means the owners can lose only the money they put in. If the assets are not enough, the owners walk away with zero. They are not asked to pay the rest out of their own pocket. An option showing equity as a minus number is wrong, even when the three numbers happen to add up to VT.

Two more limits sit underneath those checks. The senior payment can never be more than FS. The junior payment can never be more than FJ. A lender cannot collect more than it was promised.

Compare VT with FS. Then compare it with FS + FJ. That gives you the state. Then check that the three payments add to exactly VT and that equity is zero or more.

Worked example

A firm owes 60 to its senior lender and 45 to its junior lender at maturity. APR holds. After the assets are shared out, the junior lender reports that it received 18. What was the value of the firm's assets at maturity, and what did the owners receive?
1
Read what the junior payment tells you.
The junior lender received 18. That is more than nothing and less than the 45 it was promised. A part payment to the junior lender only happens in state II.
2
In state II the senior lender is paid in full.
Senior = FS = 60
The junior lender received something, so the senior lender must already be full. That is what the priority order means.
3
In state II the owners receive nothing.
Equity = 0
Money only reaches the owners once both debts are full. The junior debt is not full.
4
Use the adding-up rule backwards to find VT.
VT = 60 + 18 + 0 = 78
5
Check the answer against the borders.
FS = 60  ·  FS + FJ = 105
78 sits between 60 and 105, which is state II. That matches the part payment we started from.
The assets were worth 78 at maturity. The owners received 0.
Try it. A firm owes 80 to its senior lender and 40 to its junior lender at maturity. The assets turn out to be worth exactly 80. What does each holder receive?

Senior = Min(80, 80) = 80. The senior lender is paid in full, and not one unit more.

Junior = Min(80 − 80, 40) = Min(0, 40) = 0. There is nothing left over for it.

Equity = Max(80 − 120, 0) = 0.

Check: 80 + 0 + 0 = 80. Correct.

This is the border case. VT lands exactly on FS. The senior lender gets back exactly what it is owed, and everyone behind it gets zero. One unit less and the senior lender would also be short.

Try it. A firm owes 60 to its senior lender and 40 to its junior lender. Its assets at maturity are worth 90. A classmate answers: senior 60, junior 40, equity −10. Say what is wrong and give the right three numbers.

Two things are wrong. Equity is negative, which limited liability does not allow. And the three numbers add to 60 + 40 − 10 = 90, which looks right, so the adding-up check alone would let this pass. That is why you run both checks.

The right answer: 90 is above 60, so senior gets 60 in full. 90 is below 60 + 40 = 100, so the firm is short. Junior gets Min(90 − 60, 40) = 30. Equity gets Max(90 − 100, 0) = 0.

Check: 60 + 30 + 0 = 90. Correct.

The junior lender is 10 short of its 40. That gap is the junior lender's loss. It is not a debt the owners owe anyone.

3 · What happens when the waterfall actually runs

Default, bankruptcy, and two routes: Chapter 11 or Chapter 7 · 1:38

The formulas above describe one moment: the maturity date, with the money already counted. The exam also asks concept questions about the real events around that moment. These are short marks. Learn the words.

Default and bankruptcy

TermWhat it means
DefaultThe firm does not pay what it promised on the date it promised. In the waterfall, default is any case where a bucket is not filled.
BankruptcyThe legal process that follows when a firm cannot pay. It decides who gets what, and in what order.
Chapter 11Reorganisation. The firm keeps trading while a plan is agreed that changes the amounts and the dates of what it owes.
Chapter 7Liquidation. The firm stops. Its assets are sold and the cash is shared out in priority order.
Out of courtThe firm and its lenders agree a new deal privately, with no court. Faster and cheaper.
In courtA court runs the process. It brings legal fees, paperwork, advisers to pay and lost time. All of that comes out of the same pot that the claim holders are waiting for.

Your class notes list Chapter 11 and Chapter 7 as exam topics but do not spell the two chapters out. The two lines above give the standard meaning of each.

Court costs come out of the same pot, so APR sometimes bends · 1:41

Your class notes add one honest warning. In a real reorganisation, APR is sometimes bent. The owners can slow the process down and destroy value while they do it, so lenders may agree to leave them something to finish faster. On the exam this only matters when a question says APR holds or does not hold. Read that line.

Equity stops at zero because a corporation has limited liability. The owners can lose everything they put in. They cannot be made to pay the lenders from their own pocket.

Who runs the firm, and for whom

Owners and managers: direct and indirect agency costs · 1:37

The owners do not usually manage the firm. They hire managers. The owners are the principal and the managers are the agent. When the two want different things, that is the agency problem.

TermWhat it means
Direct agency costMoney spent on things the owners would not choose, such as a corporate jet, plus the cost of watching the managers.
Indirect agency costValue not created. A manager turns down the better but riskier project to protect his or her own position.
CompensationPay in shares and options, and the threat of losing the job. It makes the manager's wealth move with the owners' wealth.
Corporate controlThe threat of a takeover. If the share price falls, an outside buyer can take the firm and replace the managers.
Effect of outsidersLarge outside investors and analysts watch and push. Your class names CalPERS, the California state workers' fund, as this kind of corporate police.
Three tools against the agency problem · 1:40

There is a link between this panel and the waterfall. Equity gains when the assets rise and loses nothing more once they fall below the debt. That shape makes the owners keen to take risk, and it is one reason lenders write terms and watch the managers.

Worked example

A firm owes 120 in total to its lenders at maturity. Its assets are worth 95. Before any court date, the lenders and the owners meet. The lenders offer to leave the owners a small stake in the firm if the deal is signed quickly. Under strict APR, what should the owners receive, and why might the lenders still make that offer?
1
Apply APR to the numbers given.
Equity = Max(95 − 120, 0) = Max(−25, 0) = 0
Under strict APR the owners receive nothing. The lenders are 25 short.
2
Name what the lenders are buying.
A court process costs money and takes time, and those costs come out of the 95. The owners can drag the process out. Paying them a little can leave the lenders with more than a long fight would.
3
Name the event.
This is an out-of-court deal, and APR has been broken here. Your topic list calls that a deviation from APR. Both terms are on the list.
Under strict APR the owners receive 0. The lenders may still pay them something to avoid the cost and delay of an in-court process.
Try it. A chief executive turns down the project with the higher NPV because it is riskier and a bad outcome could cost her the job. Name the problem in course words, and name two tools the owners have against it.

This is the agency problem, and this form of it is an indirect agency cost. No money is spent wrongly. Value is simply not created.

Two tools. First, compensation: pay her in shares or options, so that her wealth rises and falls with the owners' wealth. Second, corporate control: the threat that an outside buyer takes the firm and replaces her. Pressure from large outside investors is a third.
