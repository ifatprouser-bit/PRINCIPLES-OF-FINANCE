DAY 4 · Tuesday 29 September

Perpetuity and growth

A payment that never stops. A payment that grows. And the one rule that decides which formula you are allowed to use.

Before you start: three streams, two that never end · 1:16
Five minutes: recall

1. A dollar you get next year is worth less than a dollar today. Name two reasons why.

Any two of these three. People prefer to spend now. To give that up they want more money later. Inflation. The same dollar buys less next year. Risk. The promised money may never arrive. All three sit inside one number: the discount rate r.

2. You will receive $5,000 in 4 years. The discount rate is 7%. What is it worth today? Then say which way the answer moves if the rate goes up to 9%.

PV = 5,000 / 1.074 = $3,814.48.
At 9%: PV = 5,000 / 1.094 = $3,542.13. It goes down. A higher rate always pushes present value down. Today's page uses this fact a lot.
Guess first

A bond pays $50 every year and it never matures, so it never pays the money back. You will collect $50 a year, and so will your children, and so will theirs. The required return is 5%.

Guess: is this bond worth more or less than $2,000 today? Write your guess and one line saying why. No answer here. This is a guess, not a test. You will know by the end of panel 1.

1 · The plain perpetuity: forever, but still a number you can name

Why a payment that never ends still has a price · 1:38

A perpetuity is the same payment, arriving after the same gap in time, that never ends. The British government once sold bonds like this, called consols. They paid a fixed coupon, which is the regular interest payment, and never paid the borrowed money back. A company can also live forever, so a fixed dividend paid by a company is close to the same shape.

"Never ends" sounds like it should be worth an endless amount of money. It is not. The reason is discounting. A payment 60 years away is worth very little today. A payment 200 years away is worth almost nothing. The payments keep coming, but the value you add for each new one keeps shrinking. The running total climbs, slows, and settles on a ceiling.

PV of a perpetuity = A / r.
A is the payment, the same size every time. r is the required return per period. The first payment comes one period from now, and the answer is the value today.
Running total of $30 received every year, discounted at 5%. Each new year adds less than the year before. The line never crosses $600.

Worked example
A university fund promises a scholarship of $9,000 at the end of every year. The promise has no end date. The required return is 6%. What is the fund worth today?
1
Pick the shape. Same amount every year, no growth, no end date. That is a plain perpetuity.
PV = A / r
2
Read the inputs. A = $9,000 a year. r = 6% = 0.06. There is no n, because there is no last year.
3
Divide.
PV = 9,000 / 0.06 = 150,000
4
Check it backwards. If you had $150,000 and it earned 6%, it would pay you 150,000 × 0.06 = $9,000 a year and the $150,000 would still be there. The answer survives the check.
The fund is worth $150,000 today.
Try it. A consol pays $75 every year forever. The required return is 5%. What is it worth? Then the market's required return rises to 6%. What is it worth now?
At 5%: 75 / 0.05 = $1,500.
At 6%: 75 / 0.06 = $1,250.
The coupon did not move. The rate moved, so the price moved the other way. Rate up, price down.
Try it. A perpetuity pays $600 a year. The required return falls from 8% to 6%. By how much does the value rise?
At 8%: 600 / 0.08 = $7,500. At 6%: 600 / 0.06 = $10,000. The value rises by $2,500.
A perpetuity has no maturity date, so there is no day when it is paid back. That is why it reacts strongly to a rate change. No final repayment arrives at the end to hold the price steady.

2 · The growing perpetuity: r must beat g

A pay rise against prices: value lives in the gap · 1:47

A growing perpetuity is a payment that never ends and gets bigger every period by the same percentage. Call that percentage g. This is the normal shape for a share of stock. Many firms raise their dividend by a few percent a year and keep doing it.

One input needs care. The top of the formula is CF1, the cash flow at the end of the first period, not the one just paid. If the question gives you last year's dividend, multiply it by (1 + g) before you divide.

When growth reaches the required return: the wall · 1:51

Now the limit on this formula. It divides by (r − g). If g gets close to r, that gap gets tiny and the value explodes. If g equals r the gap is zero and division is not possible. If g is bigger than r the formula hands you a negative number, which is nonsense for a stream of positive payments. The honest reading is this: a stream that grows forever at least as fast as you discount it is worth more than any price. No real asset does that, so in an exam this is your signal that the growing perpetuity formula is the wrong tool, or that the growth rate in the question cannot last forever.

PV of a growing perpetuity = CF1 / (r − g), and you may only use it when r > g.
CF1 is the cash flow one period from now. If the question gives CF0, then CF1 = CF0 × (1 + g).
Value of a share paying a $2.00 dividend now, growing at g forever, discounted at 9%. Near the wall the price becomes wild. A small change in g moves the answer a very long way.

Read the steep part of that curve as a warning about accuracy. When r and g are far apart, a small error in g barely matters. When they are close, a change of half a percent in g can double the answer. That is why an exam question with r just above g is testing whether you noticed how thin the gap is, not whether you can divide.

Worked example
A company has just paid a dividend of $2.00 per share. It plans to raise the dividend by 4% every year, with no end date. Investors require 9% on shares of this risk. What is one share worth today?
1
Pick the shape. Payments never end and they grow at a fixed percentage. That is a growing perpetuity.
PV = CF1 / (r − g)
2
Check the limit before anything else. r = 9%, g = 4%. r is bigger than g, so the formula is allowed.
3
Move the dividend forward one year. $2.00 was just paid, so it is CF0.
CF1 = 2.00 × 1.04 = 2.08
4
Find the gap.
r − g = 0.09 − 0.04 = 0.05
5
Divide.
PV = 2.08 / 0.05 = 41.60
6
Check it backwards. 41.60 × 0.05 = 2.08, which is next year's dividend. Correct. If you had skipped the (1 + g) and used 2.00, you would have got 40.00, which is $1.60 too low.
One share is worth $41.60 today.
Try it. A firm paid a dividend of $1.50 per share yesterday. Dividends will grow 2% a year with no end date. Investors require 8%. What is the share worth?
r = 8% is above g = 2%, so the formula is allowed.
CF1 = 1.50 × 1.02 = 1.53. Gap = 0.08 − 0.02 = 0.06.
PV = 1.53 / 0.06 = $25.50.
Try it. Same firm, same $1.50 dividend, same 2% growth. Investors now require only 6%. What is the share worth, and why did it move so much?
CF1 = 1.53 is unchanged. Gap = 0.06 − 0.02 = 0.04.
PV = 1.53 / 0.04 = $38.25. That is 50% higher than $25.50.
The required return fell by two points, but the gap (r − g) fell by a third, from 0.06 to 0.04. The gap is the whole bottom of the fraction, so the price reacts to it hard.

3 · The growing annuity: growth with a last payment

The gold mine: look for the last payment · 1:42

A growing annuity is the same idea as a growing perpetuity with one change. The payments stop. There is a fixed number of them, n. A mining permit for 20 years, a lease that runs 8 years, a patent with 12 years left: the cash flow grows, and then one day there is no more cash flow.

The choice between the two formulas is not about how the cash flow behaves. Both grow. It is about whether the stream has a last payment. Look for an end date in the question. A permit period, a contract length, a remaining life. If you find one, you need n, and you need the growing annuity.

PV of a growing annuity = CF1 × [ 1 − ((1 + g) / (1 + r))n ] / (r − g).
Use it when the payments grow and there is a last payment. Use the growing perpetuity only when nothing in the question ends the stream.
When r equals g: every payment buys the same basket · 1:36

This formula has one special case, and the course notes state it directly. When r equals g, you cannot divide by (r − g), but the present value still exists. Every payment grows at exactly the rate you discount at, so every payment is worth the same amount today: this year's cash flow. The answer is simply that amount multiplied by the number of payments.

When r = g, PV of a growing annuity = n × A, where A is this year's cash flow (the one before the first growth). No division at all.

Worked example
You hold the rights to a gold mine for the next 20 years. You plan to take out 5,000 ounces of gold every year. The price today is $300 an ounce and it is expected to rise 3% a year. The right discount rate for this project is 10%. What is the gold from this mine worth today?
1
Pick the shape. The quantity is fixed at 5,000 ounces, but the price grows 3% a year, so the yearly income grows 3% a year. The rights end after 20 years. Growth plus an end date means growing annuity.
2
Build CF1. Today's income would be 5,000 × 300 = $1,500,000. The first cash flow arrives one year from now, after one year of growth.
CF1 = 1,500,000 × 1.03 = 1,545,000
3
Read the rest of the inputs. r = 0.10, g = 0.03, n = 20. Check the limit: r is above g, and r is not equal to g, so the main formula works.
4
Do the bracket.
(1.03 / 1.10)20 = 0.268467
1 − 0.268467 = 0.731533
5
Divide by the gap.
0.731533 / 0.07 = 10.4505
This number is the value today of $1 received one year from now and growing 3% a year for 20 years.
6
Multiply by CF1.
1,545,000 × 10.4505 = 16,145,980
The gold is worth about $16,145,980 today, close to $16.15 million.

Now the reason the choice matters. Take exactly the same cash flow and treat it as a growing perpetuity by mistake, so 1,545,000 / 0.07. You get $22,071,429. That is $5.9 million too high, an error of 37%. You would pay far too much for the mine. The formula you pick is worth more than the arithmetic you do with it.

Dropping the 20 year limit adds $5.9 million of value that the contract never pays you.
Try it. A lease pays $10,000 at the end of year 1. Each later payment is 5% bigger than the one before. The lease runs 8 years and then ends. The discount rate is 9%. What is the lease worth today?
Growth plus an end date, so growing annuity. CF1 = $10,000 is already the first payment, so do not multiply by 1.05.
(1.05 / 1.09)8 = 0.741485. 1 − 0.741485 = 0.258515.
0.258515 / 0.04 = 6.462873.
PV = 10,000 × 6.462873 = $64,628.73.
Try it. Same lease, same $10,000 first payment, same 5% growth, same 9% rate. This time the lease has no end date. What is it worth, and how much of the value sits after year 8?
No end date, so growing perpetuity: 10,000 / (0.09 − 0.05) = 10,000 / 0.04 = $250,000.
Value after year 8 = 250,000 − 64,628.73 = $185,371.27, which is 74% of the total.
Compare this with panel 1. There, almost all the value sat in the first 50 years. Here it does not, because growth of 5% cancels most of the 9% discounting. The closer g is to r, the more the far future matters, and the more dangerous it is to guess at an end date.
