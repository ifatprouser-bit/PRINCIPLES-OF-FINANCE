Day 3 · Monday 28 September

Annuity: the same payment every period

One formula replaces a long column of divisions. Today you learn when you are allowed to use it.

Before you start: today's words · 1:11
Five minutes: recall

1. Give one real asset and one financial asset. What is the link between the two?

2. One of these is a size, the other is a move: the equity of a firm, and a dividend it pays. Which is which?

Say both answers out loud before you press the button.

1. A real asset is a thing that produces: land, a machine, a building. A financial asset is a claim on a real asset: a share, a bond. The financial asset does not add to the output of the economy by itself. It only says who owns the output of the real asset.

2. Equity is a size. It is what is left of the firm at one moment in time, after the debt is covered. A dividend is a move. It is cash leaving the firm and going to the shareholders. Paying a dividend makes the equity smaller. It does not create value.

Guess first

You will be paid 1,000 at the end of each year for 3 years. The rate is 10%.

Is the value of that promise today above 3,000, exactly 3,000, or below 3,000? Write your guess and one line saying why.

This is a guess, not a test. No answer is shown. You will know by the end of the page.

1. Three tests for an annuity

Two cleaning clients: which one is paying an annuity? · 1:43

An annuity is the same payment, arriving after the same gap in time, for a fixed number of periods. That one sentence hides three separate tests, and a stream has to pass all three.

TestWhat it asksFails when
Same amountIs every payment the same number?The payment grows, shrinks, or jumps once
Same gapIs the time between payments always equal?A year is skipped
Fixed countDoes it stop after a known number of payments?It runs forever. That is a perpetuity, a stream that never stops. Day 4
End of period: the first payment is a full year away · 1:33

In this course the payments arrive at the end of each period unless the question says otherwise. Every class example and every formula on the exam sheet is built that way, so read a question as end of period unless the words say start of period.

End of period is where students lose marks. The first payment arrives at the end of year 1, so it is one full year away. It is divided by (1 + r) one time, not zero times. The payment at the end of year 3 is divided three times.

Before you reach for any annuity formula, run the three tests: same amount, same gap, fixed count. If one test fails, this is not one annuity. Split the stream into parts and value each part on its own.
Three payments of 500, a rate of 6%. The count of divisions equals the year number.

Worked example

A cleaning contract pays you 500 at the end of each year for the next 3 years. Your discount rate is 6%. What is the stream worth today? Do it the long way, one payment at a time.
1
Name the tool.
Three single cash flows discounted back to time 0, then added. Cash flows from different dates cannot be added until they sit on the same date.
2
The payment at the end of year 1.
500 / 1.06 = 471.70
3
The payment at the end of year 2.
500 / 1.062 = 500 / 1.1236 = 445.00
4
The payment at the end of year 3.
500 / 1.063 = 500 / 1.191016 = 419.81
5
Add the three, now that they all sit on today.
471.70 + 445.00 + 419.81 = 1,336.51
Answer: 1,336.51. It is below 1,500, which is what you would get by adding the raw payments. Waiting costs value.
Try it. A bond pays 40 at the end of each year for 10 years, and also returns 1,000 at the end of year 10. Is this whole stream one annuity?
No. The 40 payments pass all three tests, so they are an annuity of 10 payments. The 1,000 at year 10 is a single cash flow that arrives once. Value the two parts separately and add them. You meet this exact shape again on day 6.
Try it. A payment of 800 arrives at the end of year 4. The rate is 6%. How many times is it divided by 1.06, and what is it worth today?
Four times, because it sits at the end of year 4. 800 / 1.064 = 800 / 1.262477 = 633.67. A common slip is three divisions, which gives 671.70 and is too high.

2. Present value of an annuity

Why equal payments are worth less than their total · 1:44

The long way works, but on a 20 year stream it is 20 divisions with a scientific calculator and no room for a mistake. The annuity formula does the whole column in one line.

PV = A × [ 1 − 1 / (1 + r)n ] / r

A is the payment, the same size every time. r is the rate per period. n is the number of payments. The part in the brackets, divided by r, is called the annuity factor. It is the value today of 1 received at the end of every period for n periods. You then multiply it by the real payment.

The copier: pay cash now or pay every year? · 1:42

The answer sits at time 0, which is one full period before the first payment. That is built into the formula. You do not discount the result again.

Use PV of an annuity when a fixed number of equal payments starts at the end of the next period. The answer is dated one period before the first payment, so it is already today's value.

Read across a row: a higher rate makes the same stream worth less. Read down a column: more payments are worth more, but each extra one adds less than the one before. You will not get this table in the exam, so work the factor out yourself. The table is here to show the shape.

Worked example

Your firm can buy a copier for 10,000 cash today, or pay 3,000 at the end of each year for 5 years. Your opportunity cost, the rate you could earn elsewhere, is 12%. Which should you choose?
1
Name the formula.
The yearly payments are 5 equal payments, one year apart, ending after year 5. All three tests pass, so it is an annuity. Use PV = A × [1 − 1/(1+r)n] / r.
2
Take each input out of the question.
A = 3,000. r = 0.12. n = 5. The 10,000 is not an input. It is the price you compare against.
3
Raise 1 + r to the power n.
1.125 = 1.762342
4
Take one over that, then take it away from 1.
1 / 1.762342 = 0.567427  →  1 − 0.567427 = 0.432573
5
Divide by r. This is the annuity factor.
0.432573 / 0.12 = 3.604776
6
Multiply by the payment.
3,000 × 3.604776 = 10,814.33
7
Check the answer the long way.
2,678.57 + 2,391.58 + 2,135.34 + 1,906.55 + 1,702.28 = 10,814.33
The five separate divisions give the same number, so the factor was right.
Answer: paying year by year costs 10,814.33 in today's money. That is more than 10,000, so pay the 10,000 cash now.
Try it. A client will pay you 4,000 at the end of each year for 6 years. Your rate is 7%. What is the stream worth today? Then the rate falls to 4%. What is it worth now, and did it rise or fall?
At 7%: factor = (1 − 1/1.076) / 0.07 = 4.766540, so PV = 4,000 × 4.766540 = 19,066.16.
At 4%: factor = (1 − 1/1.046) / 0.04 = 5.242137, so PV = 4,000 × 5.242137 = 20,968.55.
It rose by 1,902.39. A lower rate means waiting costs you less, so the same promise is worth more today.
Try it, the other way round. You borrow 25,000 today and repay it with one equal payment at the end of each of the next 5 years. The rate is 8%. How big is each payment?
Here the PV is given and A is unknown, so divide instead of multiply. Factor = (1 − 1/1.085) / 0.08 = 3.992710. A = 25,000 / 3.992710 = 6,261.41 a year. Check: 6,261.41 × 3.992710 = 25,000.

3. Future value of an annuity

Saving for retirement: what equal payments grow into · 1:42

Sometimes you do not want today's value. You want the pile of money at the end, after saving the same amount every year. That is the future value of an annuity.

FV = A × [ (1 + r)n − 1 ] / r

Timing works the same way, but in the other direction. The formula lands on the date of the last payment. The payment made at the end of year 1 has n − 1 years left to earn interest. The final payment has just arrived, so it earns nothing at all.

FV of an annuity is dated on the day of the last payment. The last payment earns no interest. If you give it a year of interest you get an answer that is too high by a factor of (1 + r).
Saving 2,000 at the end of each year for 3 years at 8%. The last 2,000 has had no time to grow.

Worked example

A saver puts 2,000 into a retirement account at the end of every year. She starts at age 25 and stops at age 65, so she makes 40 payments. The account earns 8% a year. How much is in it on the day of the last payment?
1
Name the formula.
Forty equal payments, one year apart, a known end date, and we want the value at the end. Use FV = A × [(1+r)n − 1] / r.
2
Take each input out of the question.
A = 2,000. r = 0.08. n = 40. Her age is only there to tell you n.
3
Raise 1 + r to the power n.
1.0840 = 21.724521
4
Take away 1, then divide by r.
21.724521 − 1 = 20.724521  →  20.724521 / 0.08 = 259.056519
5
Multiply by the payment.
2,000 × 259.056519 = 518,113.04
6
Check that the size is believable.
40 × 2,000 = 80,000 paid in. 518,113.04 − 80,000 = 438,113.04 of interest.
Over 40 years at 8% the interest should be much bigger than the deposits, and it is. If your answer had been near 80,000 you forgot to compound, that is, to earn interest on top of earlier interest.
Answer: 518,113.04 on the day of the last payment.
Try it. You save 1,500 at the end of each year for 10 years at 5%. How much is there right after the tenth payment?
1.0510 = 1.628895. Take away 1 to get 0.628895, divide by 0.05 to get 12.577893. Then 1,500 × 12.577893 = 18,866.84. You paid in 15,000, so 3,866.84 is interest.
Try it, the other way round. You need 60,000 in 8 years. The account pays 6%. How much must you put in at the end of each year?
The FV is given and A is unknown, so divide. Factor = (1.068 − 1) / 0.06 = 9.897468. A = 60,000 / 9.897468 = 6,062.16 a year. Check: 6,062.16 × 9.897468 = 60,000.
The one thing to carry into tomorrow
  • PV of an annuity is dated one period before the first payment.
  • FV of an annuity is dated on the last payment.
  • Both formulas assume the payment lands at the end of each period.
