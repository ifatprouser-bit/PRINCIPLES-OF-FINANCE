DAY 5 · Wednesday 30 September

Mixed streams

A messy stream is only a set of small streams sitting side by side. Cut it, value each piece, add.

Five minutes: recall

Day 3 was the annuity. Answer both on paper before you open anything.

1. What has to be true about a run of payments before you are allowed to call it an annuity?

2. You will receive 1,000 at the end of each year for 3 years. The rate is 10 percent. What is the value today, and what will the money be worth at the end of year 3?

Write your two answers down, then check.

1. Three things. The payment is the same size every time. The gaps between payments are equal, one per period. The run stops after a fixed number of periods. If any one of those fails, it is not an annuity and the annuity formula is the wrong tool.

2. Value today: 1,000 × (1 − 1.10−3) / 0.10 = 2,486.85

Value at the end of year 3: 1,000 × (1.103 − 1) / 0.10 = 3,310.00

Check: 2,486.85 × 1.103 = 3,310.00. The two answers are the same money seen from two dates.

Guess first

A contract pays 750 at the end of year 4, 750 at the end of year 5 and 750 at the end of year 6. Nothing before, nothing after. The rate is 10 percent.

You put 750, n = 3 and 10 percent into the annuity formula. You get a number. Which year does that number belong to?

Guess now. No answer is shown here. This is a guess, not a test. The whole page answers it.

1. Cut the stream into pieces you already know

Why this day carries the most weight

In his guide for the final, the professor writes: "Most probable question on the test: I will give a stream of future cash flows that is composed of simple cash flows and annuities and will ask for the present value of these cash flows."

In plain words: he plans to hand you a mix of one-off payments and a run of equal payments, and ask what the whole thing is worth today. That is exactly this page.

You already have every tool you need. Day 2 gave you the single cash flow. Day 3 gave you the annuity. Day 4 gave you the perpetuity and growth. Nothing new is added today. What is new is the cutting: seeing a long list of numbers and spotting the two or three familiar shapes hiding inside it.

The reason you cannot just add the numbers is the rule from day 2. Money that arrives in different years cannot be added. First bring every amount to the same date. Then add.

MoveWhat you do
1. DrawDraw the timeline. Mark every year that has money on it. Write the amount above the year.
2. NameLook at the picture and name each piece: a single cash flow, an annuity, or a perpetuity, which is a stream that never stops.
3. ValueValue each piece with its own formula. Then write down which year that value sits in. This line is where marks are lost.
4. AddBring any piece that is not yet at year 0 back to year 0. Now every piece sits on the same date, so you may add them.

One stream, two pieces. The four purple bars repeat and are equally spaced, so they are an annuity. The amber bar stands alone, so it is a single cash flow.

Never add amounts from different years. Value each piece first, carry every piece back to year 0, and only then add.

Worked example

A supplier will pay you 2,000 at the end of each year for the next 4 years. On top of that you will receive one payment of 9,000 at the end of year 5. The required rate of return is 7 percent. What is the whole stream worth today?
1
Draw and name the pieces.
Piece A: 2,000 in years 1, 2, 3, 4. Same size, one per year, four of them. That is an annuity. Piece B: 9,000 in year 5 on its own. That is a single cash flow.
2
Value piece A with the annuity formula.
PV = A × (1 − (1+r)−n) / r
PV = 2,000 × (1 − 1.07−4) / 0.07
3
Do the bracket first.
1.074 = 1.310796 → 1 / 1.310796 = 0.762895
(1 − 0.762895) / 0.07 = 3.387211
4
Multiply by the payment.
2,000 × 3.387211 = 6,774.42
The first payment of piece A lands at year 1, so this value already sits at year 0. No extra move is needed here. Panel 2 is about the case where a move is needed.
5
Value piece B with the single cash flow formula.
PV = CF / (1+r)t = 9,000 / 1.075 = 9,000 / 1.402552 = 6,416.88
6
Both pieces now sit at year 0, so add them.
6,774.42 + 6,416.88 = 13,191.30
The stream is worth 13,191.30 today.

Sense check. The cash actually paid is 8,000 plus 9,000, which is 17,000. Your answer must be smaller than that, because the money arrives later. 13,191.30 is smaller. It must also be bigger than the largest single piece. It is.

Try it. You will receive 650 at the end of each year for 3 years, plus a single 6,000 at the end of year 3. The rate is 4 percent. What is the stream worth today?

Annuity piece: 650 × (1 − 1.04−3) / 0.04 = 650 × 2.775091 = 1,803.81. First payment is at year 1, so this already sits at year 0.

Single piece: 6,000 / 1.043 = 6,000 / 1.124864 = 5,333.98.

Total: 1,803.81 + 5,333.98 = 7,137.79.

Note that the 6,000 and the third 650 both land in year 3. That is allowed. Two pieces may share a year. You still value them separately.

2. Where the annuity value lands, and why you must move it again

This is the idea that decides your mark on this topic. Read it twice.

The annuity formula does not give you a value at year 0. It gives you a value one period before the first payment. When the first payment is at year 1, one period before that is year 0, which is today, so nothing more is needed. That is the case everyone practices, and it hides the rule.

When the first payment is later, the formula still lands one period before it. So the number you get sits in some future year, not today. It is a future value. You then have to discount that number back to year 0, that is, bring it back to what it is worth today, with the single cash flow formula. Two moves, not one.

A run of equal payments that starts later than year 1 is called a delayed annuity. The same rule applies to a delayed perpetuity: the perpetuity formula also lands one period before the first payment.

Count n as the number of payments. The annuity value lands in the year just before the first payment. If that year is not 0, discount it again by that many years.

Two hops, not one. The amber hop is the annuity formula and it stops at year 3. The red hop is the second discounting that finally reaches today. Stopping after the amber hop is the classic lost mark.

Worked example

A permit will pay you 4,000 at the end of year 4, and the same 4,000 at the end of years 5, 6 and 7. You receive nothing in years 1, 2 and 3, and nothing after year 7. The rate is 9 percent. What is the permit worth today?
1
Draw it and count the payments.
Money lands in years 4, 5, 6, 7. Four equal payments, one per year. So n = 4, not 7. n counts payments, never years since today.
2
Find the year the annuity value will land in.
The first payment is at year 4. One period before year 4 is year 3. So the annuity formula will give a value dated year 3.
3
Hop 1. Use the annuity formula.
V3 = 4,000 × (1 − 1.09−4) / 0.09
1.094 = 1.411582 → 1 / 1.411582 = 0.708425
(1 − 0.708425) / 0.09 = 3.239720
4,000 × 3.239720 = 12,958.88
4
Say out loud what that number is.
12,958.88 is what the four payments are worth standing at the end of year 3. It is not the answer. It is money sitting three years in the future.
5
Hop 2. Discount that number back 3 years.
PV = 12,958.88 / 1.093 = 12,958.88 / 1.295029 = 10,006.63
The permit is worth 10,006.63 today.

Check it the long way. Discount the four payments one at a time: 4,000/1.094 = 2,833.70, 4,000/1.095 = 2,599.73, 4,000/1.096 = 2,385.07, 4,000/1.097 = 2,188.14. Add them without rounding on the way: 10,006.63. Same answer. The two hops are just a fast way of doing those four divisions at once.

The trap in numbers. If you stop after hop 1 you write 12,958.88. That is 2,952.25 too much, about 30 percent too high. On a multiple choice paper that wrong number will be sitting there as an option, because it is the mistake the question is built to catch.

Try it. A rental agreement pays 1,800 at the end of each year from year 3 to year 8, and nothing else. The rate is 12 percent. What is it worth today?

Payments land in years 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8. That is six payments, so n = 6.

First payment is year 3, so the annuity value lands at year 2.

Hop 1: 1,800 × (1 − 1.12−6) / 0.12 = 1,800 × 4.111407 = 7,400.53 at year 2.

Hop 2: 7,400.53 / 1.122 = 7,400.53 / 1.2544 = 5,899.66 today.

If you stopped at 7,400.53 you were 1,500.87 too high.

Try it, no calculator. A fund pays 2,200 at the end of each year from year 10 to year 15. What is n, and which year does the annuity formula's answer belong to?

Years 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15. Count them: n = 6.

The first payment is at year 10, so the value lands at the end of year 9. To reach today you then divide by (1+r)9, not by (1+r)10.

The most common slip here is dividing by (1+r)10. That discounts the money one year too much and gives an answer that is too small.

3. Put the pieces together, then check

Now the real exam shape: some one-off payments, a run of equal payments in the middle, and a gap year with nothing in it. Nothing here is new. You are only doing panel 1 and panel 2 at the same time.

A gap year is not a problem. A year with no money simply has no piece attached to it. Do not try to stretch an annuity over a gap. If the payments stop and start again, that is two pieces, not one.

Keep a small table while you work: piece, value, year that value sits in, extra discounting needed. The table is what stops the delayed piece from slipping through.

Worked example

A project pays 1,000 at the end of year 1. It pays nothing in year 2. It then pays 2,500 at the end of each year from year 3 to year 7. Finally it pays 12,000 at the end of year 8. The required rate of return is 6 percent. What is the present value, meaning the value today, of the whole stream?
1
Name the pieces.
Piece A: single 1,000 at year 1. Piece B: annuity of 2,500 for years 3 to 7, so n = 5. Piece C: single 12,000 at year 8. Year 2 is empty and gets no piece.
2
Piece A.
1,000 / 1.06 = 943.40  (already at year 0)
3
Piece B, hop 1. First payment is year 3, so the value will land at year 2.
V2 = 2,500 × (1 − 1.06−5) / 0.06
1.065 = 1.338226 → (1 − 0.747258) / 0.06 = 4.212364
2,500 × 4.212364 = 10,530.91  (dated year 2)
4
Piece B, hop 2. Bring it back 2 years.
10,530.91 / 1.062 = 10,530.91 / 1.1236 = 9,372.47
5
Piece C.
12,000 / 1.068 = 12,000 / 1.593848 = 7,528.95
6
All three now sit at year 0. Add.
943.40 + 9,372.47 + 7,528.95 = 17,844.82
The stream is worth 17,844.82 today.

The fourth column is the one that saves you. If a row shows a year other than 0, that row is not finished yet.

Sense check. The cash paid out is 1,000 + 12,500 + 12,000 = 25,500. The answer must be below that and it is. It must also be above the biggest single piece, 9,372.47, and it is.

Try it. Same project, same dates, but the required rate of return rises from 6 percent to 9 percent. First say whether the value goes up or down. Then work it out.

It goes down. A higher rate punishes future money harder, so every piece shrinks.

Piece A: 1,000 / 1.09 = 917.43

Piece B, hop 1: 2,500 × (1 − 1.09−5) / 0.09 = 2,500 × 3.889651 = 9,724.13 at year 2.

Piece B, hop 2: 9,724.13 / 1.092 = 9,724.13 / 1.1881 = 8,184.60

Piece C: 12,000 / 1.098 = 12,000 / 1.992563 = 6,022.40

Total: 917.43 + 8,184.60 + 6,022.40 = 15,124.43, down from 17,844.82.

Carry these four lines into the exam
  • Draw the timeline before you touch the calculator. Every time.
  • n is the number of payments, not the number of years since today.
  • The annuity or perpetuity value lands one period before the first payment. Write that year down.
  • Add only after every piece sits at year 0.
