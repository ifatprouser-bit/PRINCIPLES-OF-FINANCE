NOA: A woman buys a coffee machine for her small cafe. All year it brings in cash. By the end of the year it's second-hand and would sell for less than she paid. Did she do well or badly? Take a moment.

TOM: Well, surely. The cash is the point. A machine getting older is what machines do.

NOA: Then you've said the thing most people miss about a share. Two separate things reach you, and you count both. The cash while you hold it, and the change in what the thing itself is worth.

TOM: And they can pull opposite ways.

NOA: Often they do. Same shape, new setting. You let out a room. Rent arrives monthly and you can spend it. The apartment's price moves meanwhile, and that number touches nothing until you sell.

TOM: Third one. A car bought for deliveries. Fees in your hand each week, resale price drifting down quietly behind them.

NOA: In all three, one part you can spend now, one that becomes real only when you let the thing go. For a share, the spendable part is the dividend. The other part is the price.

TOM: So a share whose price fell could still have paid me well.

NOA: If the dividend beat the fall, yes. And the mirror case catches as many people. A share that climbed beautifully and paid no dividend has given you nothing to spend this year.

TOM: The gain is real. It just hasn't arrived.

NOA: Last piece, and it's where the marks are. Neither part means much until you ask, against what. Two traders make the same profit on a market day, and one tied up a fortune to do it.

TOM: Same profit, one far better day. So I judge both parts against what I paid at the start, not what it's worth at the end.

NOA: The start. Always. That's the money you actually risked.

NOA: Take a breath and recall one thing. A share pays you twice, in cash and in price. Count both, against what you paid.
