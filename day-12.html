Nine tasks, one per learn day

Paper and a scientific calculator. About five minutes each. Write the answer down before you open the box.

Day 1 · What kind of thing is this number

A firm owns a factory and machines worth 840,000. It owes a bank 510,000.
(a) Of the three things named, which are real assets and which is financial, and for whom?
(b) The board pays a cash dividend of 90,000. What is the equity worth before, and after?
(c) A week later two funds trade 20,000 of the firm's shares with each other at 14 a share. How much of that money reaches the firm?

(a) The factory and the machines are real assets. They produce. The bank loan is a financial asset, and it belongs to the bank. To the firm the same loan is money it must pay back.

(b) Equity before: 840,000 − 510,000 = 330,000.

The cash leaves, so the assets fall: 840,000 − 90,000 = 750,000. The debt is untouched.

Equity after: 750,000 − 510,000 = 240,000.

Check the owners: 240,000 of equity plus 90,000 in the pocket = 330,000, exactly what they held before. The dividend moved value. It did not create any.

(c) 20,000 × 14 = 280,000 changes hands, and the firm receives 0. This is the secondary market. The firm is not part of the deal.

Day 2 · A single cash flow

You will receive 18,000 at the end of year 7. The rate is 6 percent a year.
(a) What is that payment worth today?
(b) Put your answer from (a) in the bank today at 6 percent and leave it for 7 years. What do you hold then?
(c) A different seller offers you the same 18,000 in 7 years for 9,500 today. What yearly return would that give you?

(a) One payment, one date, so PV = FV / (1 + r)N.

1.067 = 1.503630, and 18,000 / 1.503630 = 11,971.03

(b) 11,971.03 × 1.503630 = 18,000. The same money seen from the other end. Discounting and compounding are one move in two directions.

(c) Both ends and the years are known, so solve for the rate: r = (18,000 / 9,500)1/7 − 1

18,000 / 9,500 = 1.894737, then the seventh root is 1.095594, so r = 9.56 percent.

Check: 9,500 × 1.0955947 = 18,000. The trap is dividing 1.894737 by 7. The years compound, so they do not simply add up.

Day 3 · Annuity

A contract pays 7,500 at the end of each year for 11 years. The rate is 5 percent.
(a) What is the contract worth today?
(b) Instead of the contract, you save 7,500 at the end of each year for 11 years at 5 percent. How much sits there on the day of the eleventh payment?
(c) You borrow 45,000 today and repay it with 11 equal payments, one at the end of each year, at 5 percent. How big is each payment?

(a) Same amount, same gap, fixed count, so it is an annuity. PV = A × [1 − (1 + r)−n] / r

Factor: (1 − 1.05−11) / 0.05 = 8.306414

7,500 × 8.306414 = 62,298.11

(b) Now you want the pile at the end, so FV = A × [(1 + r)n − 1] / r

Factor: (1.0511 − 1) / 0.05 = 14.206787, so 7,500 × 14.206787 = 106,550.90

You paid in 82,500, so 24,050.90 is interest. The eleventh payment earns nothing, because it arrives on the day you are measuring.

(c) Here the present value is given and the payment is missing, so divide instead of multiply.

45,000 / 8.306414 = 5,417.50 a year.

Check: 5,417.50 × 8.306414 = 45,000.

Day 4 · Perpetuity and growth

The required return is 7 percent in all three parts. The first payment is always 4,200 at the end of next year.
(a) The payment is 4,200 every year and never changes or stops. What is it worth today?
(b) The payment rises 3 percent every year after the first one, and still never stops. What is it worth today?
(c) Same 3 percent rise, but there are only 10 payments in total. What is it worth today?

(a) Plain perpetuity: 4,200 / 0.07 = 60,000

(b) Growing perpetuity. Check the border first: r is 7 percent and g is 3 percent, so r is bigger than g and the formula is allowed.

4,200 / (0.07 − 0.03) = 4,200 / 0.04 = 105,000

The 4,200 is already next year's payment, so it is not grown again.

(c) Growth plus a last payment means growing annuity: CF1 × [1 − ((1 + g) / (1 + r))n] / (r − g)

(1.03 / 1.07)10 = 0.683179

1 − 0.683179 = 0.316821, then 0.316821 / 0.04 = 7.920526

4,200 × 7.920526 = 33,266.21

Read the three answers together. Stopping the stream after 10 payments removes 105,000 − 33,266.21 = 71,733.79 of value. The end date is worth more than the arithmetic.

Day 5 · Mixed streams

A project pays 3,000 at the end of year 1. It pays nothing in year 2. It then pays 5,000 at the end of each year from year 3 to year 6. Last, it pays 20,000 at the end of year 7. The rate is 8 percent. What is the whole stream worth today?

Three pieces. Year 2 is empty, so it gets no piece.

Piece A, single 3,000 at year 1. 3,000 / 1.08 = 2,777.78

Piece B, annuity of 5,000 in years 3 to 6. That is four payments, so n = 4. The first payment is at year 3, so the annuity value lands at year 2.

Hop 1: (1 − 1.08−4) / 0.08 = 3.312127, so 5,000 × 3.312127 = 16,560.63 dated year 2.

Hop 2: 16,560.63 / 1.082 = 16,560.63 / 1.1664 = 14,198.07

Piece C, single 20,000 at year 7. 1.087 = 1.713824, so 20,000 / 1.713824 = 11,669.81

Add, now that all three sit at year 0. 2,777.78 + 14,198.07 + 11,669.81 = 28,645.66

Sense check: the cash actually paid is 3,000 plus four payments of 5,000 plus 20,000, which is 43,000. The answer must be below that, and it is. Stopping after hop 1 on piece B would have left you 2,362.56 too high.

Day 6 · Bonds

A bond has a par value of 1,000 and a coupon rate of 8 percent paid once a year. It has 6 years left to maturity. Buyers of bonds of this risk ask for 5 percent.
(a) Before any arithmetic, is the price above par, below par or at par? Name that kind of bond.
(b) Work the price out.
(c) Buyers then start to ask for 10 percent. Work out the new price and name the bond again.

(a) The coupon rate of 8 percent is above the yield of 5 percent, so the price is above par. It is a premium bond.

(b) The coupon payment is 0.08 × 1,000 = 80 a year. Six coupons are an annuity, and the par value is a single cash flow at year 6.

80 × (1 − 1.05−6) / 0.05 = 80 × 5.075692 = 406.06

1,000 / 1.056 = 1,000 / 1.340096 = 746.22

406.06 + 746.22 = 1,152.27. Above par, just as part (a) said.

(c) The coupon stays at 80. Only the discount rate changes.

80 × (1 − 1.10−6) / 0.10 = 80 × 4.355261 = 348.42

1,000 / 1.106 = 1,000 / 1.771561 = 564.47

348.42 + 564.47 = 912.89. Now the coupon rate of 8 percent is below the yield of 10 percent, so it is a discount bond.

One bond, two prices, one direction. Rates up, price down.

Day 7 · Who gets paid first

A firm owes 90 to its senior lender and 60 to its junior lender at maturity. APR holds. Work out what each of the three holders receives when the assets at maturity turn out to be worth 75, then 130, then 150. Check each set of three against the value of the assets.

Write the two borders down first: FS = 90, and FS + FJ = 150. Everything follows from where VT sits against those two numbers.

VT = 75. 75 is below 90, so this is state I. Senior = Min(75, 90) = 75. Junior = 0, because VT is below FS. Equity = Max(75 − 150, 0) = 0. Check: 75 + 0 + 0 = 75.

VT = 130. 130 is above 90 but below 150, so this is state II. Senior = 90 in full. Junior = Min(130 − 90, 60) = Min(40, 60) = 40. Equity = Max(130 − 150, 0) = 0. Check: 90 + 40 + 0 = 130.

VT = 150. This is the border. Senior = 90. Junior = Min(150 − 90, 60) = Min(60, 60) = 60, paid in full and not one unit more. Equity = Max(150 − 150, 0) = 0. Check: 90 + 60 + 0 = 150.

At 150 both lenders are paid in full and the owners still get nothing. One unit more and every extra unit would go to the owners.

Day 8 · What the shareholder gets

(a) A share is bought for 150. A year later the price is 144, and the company paid a dividend of 10.50 during the year. What is the holding period return?
(b) A preferred share pays a fixed 4.50 at the end of every year and never raises it. Investors ask for 9 percent. What is it worth?
(c) A company paid a dividend of 2.40 a share yesterday. The dividend will grow 3 percent a year with no end date. Investors ask for 11 percent. What is the share worth today?

(a) r = (P1 − P0 + D1) / P0

Price change: 144 − 150 = −6. Add the dividend: −6 + 10.50 = 4.50.

4.50 / 150 = 0.03, which is 3 percent.

Split check: dividend part 10.50 / 150 = 7 percent, price part −6 / 150 = −4 percent, and 7 − 4 = 3. A falling price still left a gain, because the dividend was bigger than the fall.

(b) A fixed payment with no end date is a perpetuity: 4.50 / 0.09 = 50.00

(c) Growing perpetuity, and r is above g, so the formula is allowed. The 2.40 was paid yesterday, so grow it once.

D1 = 2.40 × 1.03 = 2.472

2.472 / (0.11 − 0.03) = 2.472 / 0.08 = 30.90

Using 2.40 on top would have given 30.00. That one slip is the most common on this formula.

Day 9 · Projects, NPV and IRR

The required return is 9 percent.
(a) Project one costs 24,000 today. It returns 9,000 at the end of year 1, 11,000 at the end of year 2 and 10,000 at the end of year 3. What is its NPV, and what is the decision?
(b) Project two costs 7,000 today and returns 7,500 at the end of year 1. Taking one project does not block the other, and there is no budget limit. What is its NPV, and what is the decision?
(c) What is the IRR of project two, and does it agree with (b)?

(a) Three different amounts, so three single cash flows. NPV = Σ CFi / (1 + r)i − I0

9,000 / 1.09 = 8,256.88

11,000 / 1.1881 = 9,258.48

10,000 / 1.295029 = 7,721.83

Total = 25,237.20, so NPV = 25,237.20 − 24,000 = 1,237.20. Above zero, so accept.

Keep the full digits on the calculator. Rounding each line first loses a cent here.

(b) 7,500 / 1.09 = 6,880.73, so NPV = 6,880.73 − 7,000 = −119.27. Below zero, so reject.

The projects are independent, so each is judged on its own. No budget limit is not the same as no test.

(c) For a one year project the IRR is the plain return: 7,500 / 7,000 − 1 = 0.071429, which is 7.14 percent.

7.14 percent is below the required 9 percent, so the IRR rejects it too. On a normal project the two rules always agree.
