NOA: Start with one payment. Not the stream, one payment, and it arrives in the year three thousand. Somebody's great great grandchild collects it. What would you hand over today for that one payment? Take a moment.

TOM: Almost nothing. I wouldn't cross the room for it.

NOA: Then you already have the answer to the bigger question. A stream that never ends is still worth an ordinary amount of money.

TOM: Say that so I can believe it, because it sounds wrong.

NOA: Think about charging a phone. Empty to nearly full, quickly. Then the last part crawls. Leave it plugged in all night and it never goes past full. The charger never stops giving. The number stops climbing.

TOM: So the payments keep coming, each one adds less than the one before, and the total stops moving.

NOA: Exactly. Picture the running total as a line. It climbs steeply, then bends, then goes so nearly flat you can't see it rise. And it never touches the ceiling above it. A century of payments is almost the whole value. The rest of forever is loose change.

TOM: And people really bought these.

NOA: The British government sold them. A fixed coupon every year, and the coupon is just the interest payment. The loan itself was never paid back. Consols. They traded at perfectly ordinary prices. Same shape as a university fund paying a scholarship with no end date, or an apartment you rent out and never mean to sell.

TOM: In all three, the value is the next payment divided by the return people want.

NOA: That's the formula in words. And watch what falls out of it. Demand a higher return, and you're dividing the same payment by a bigger number.

TOM: So the price drops. The rent never moved, but the apartment is worth less.

NOA: Take a breath and recall one thing. A payment that never ends still has a price you can put a number on, because money far enough away is worth almost nothing today.
