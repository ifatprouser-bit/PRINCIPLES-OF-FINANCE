NOA: Your neighbour asks to borrow money for a new washing machine. You say yes, and you write two things on a scrap of paper. What two things?

NOA: Take a moment. Picture what you'd actually write down.

TOM: How much. And when I get it back.

NOA: That scrap of paper is the whole idea. How much, and when. Both settled before you hand over a single coin.

TOM: So there's nothing to find out later. That's the opposite of owning a share, where nobody ever told me what I'd get.

NOA: Which is why this family is called fixed income. The payments are fixed before you buy. Second story, and you've lived this one. You open a savings account at a fixed rate for two years. The bank tells you today what it'll pay you, and on which dates.

TOM: Written down before my money goes in. Same scrap of paper, different room.

NOA: One thing is missing though. Try handing that savings account to your sister.

TOM: I can't. It has my name on it.

NOA: And that missing thing is what makes a bond a bond. Third story. A landlord rents out an apartment, fixed rent, three years signed. Then she sells the building.

TOM: And the tenant keeps paying the same rent, just to a new owner. The promise moved with the building.

NOA: That's a bond. A promise with dates on it that you can pass to somebody else.

TOM: And in real life the borrower isn't my neighbour. It's a company, or a government, needing more than any one person can lend. So it borrows a little from thousands of us, and each of us holds a slip saying how much and when.

NOA: Each slip is a bond. The same scrap of paper, printed many times over, and every one of them sellable. And from your side the money moves in two directions. Out once, when you pay for the paper. In afterwards, again and again.

TOM: Out once, in many times.

NOA: Walk the years across and you see it. A small amount arriving at the end of each year. Then at the far end one much taller arrival, because the final year brings the last payment and the whole borrowed sum together.

TOM: And the price of the paper is just what all of that is worth today.

NOA: Nothing stranger than that. Which leaves one question, always, before any arithmetic. What shape do the promises make? One lump at the end and nothing before it. Or a small amount every year plus the lump at the end. Or a small amount every year that never stops, and the borrowed sum that never comes back at all.

TOM: Three shapes, and each one is a tool I already have. Pay once. Pay the same every year. Pay forever.

NOA: Say the shape out loud first, and the formula chooses itself.

NOA: Take a breath and recall one thing. A bond is how much, and when, on paper you can sell. Name the shape before you name a formula.
