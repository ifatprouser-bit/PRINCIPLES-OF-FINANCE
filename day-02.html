DAY 2 · Sunday 27 September

A single cash flow

One payment, one date. Move it to today, move it to the future, and read the rate that links the two.

Before you start: today's words · 1:06
Five minutes: recall

1. Sort these four into real assets and financial assets: a delivery truck, a corporate bond, a bakery oven, a share of stock.

2. Equity is a size and a dividend is a move. Say in one line what each of those two words is measuring.

Answer both out loud before you open the box.

1. Real assets: the delivery truck and the bakery oven. They are the things that actually produce goods and services. Financial assets: the corporate bond and the share of stock. They are paper claims on the cash that the real assets produce.

2. Equity is a size. It is how big the owners' claim is at one moment in time. A dividend is a move. It is cash leaving the firm and going to the owners, so it makes the equity smaller.

Guess first

A customer owes you 1,000 and wants to pay in two years instead of today. Money earns 6 percent a year.

What single number in two years would feel equal to 1,000 today? Write your guess on paper now.

This is a guess, not a test. No answer is shown here. You will be able to check it yourself by the end of the page.

1. You cannot add money from two different dates

Two offers on two dates: why you can't compare raw numbers · 1:40

This is the idea the professor puts first, and everything from here to day 9 stands on it. A payment of 100 today and a payment of 100 in three years are not the same thing. They are not even the same kind of thing.

Think of dates as currencies. Money in 2029 is one currency. Money today is another. You would never add 100 dollars to 100 shekels and write 200. You change one into the other first, at the exchange rate. The interest rate is that exchange rate.

Three reasons a payment in the future is worth less than the same payment today:

WaitingPeople prefer to spend now. To give that up they want more back later. That is the real rate.
InflationPrices rise, so the same money buys less later.
RiskThe payment may arrive smaller, or late, or never.

Your course file writes the first two together as R = real rate + expected inflation and then adds a premium, which is extra return, if the investor is carrying risk. You do not need to split the rate on the exam. You need to know that all three reasons sit inside the one number r.

Before any formula, draw the timeline. Mark today as 0, mark every payment on its own date, and never add two numbers that sit on different dates.

The payment stays where it is. Only its value moves, and it moves one period at a time.

Worked example

A supplier offers you two ways to be paid for the same job. Offer A is 1,000 paid today. Offer B is 1,080 paid in one year. Money is worth 5 percent a year to you. Which offer is bigger, and by how much?
1
Put both offers on a timeline.
Offer A sits at date 0. Offer B sits at date 1. They are on different dates, so you cannot compare the raw numbers 1,000 and 1,080.
2
Choose one date to compare on. Today is the easy one.
Offer A is already at today, so it needs no change.
3
Bring offer B back one year.
PV = 1,080 / (1 + 0.05) = 1,028.57
4
Now both numbers sit on the same date, so you may compare them.
1,028.57 − 1,000 = 28.57
Offer B is bigger, by about 28.57 in today's money. Check it the other way: 1,028.57 × 1.05 = 1,080, so the two really are the same money seen from two dates.
Try it. Same two offers, 1,000 today or 1,080 in one year. At what interest rate would the two offers feel exactly equal?
Equal means 1,000 × (1 + r) = 1,080. So r = 1,080 / 1,000 − 1 = 0.08, that is 8 percent.
Read it this way: below 8 percent the later offer wins, above 8 percent the money-today offer wins.

2. Present value: pull one payment back to today

Infosoft's payment: what a future payment is worth today · 1:29

A single cash flow is one payment on one future date. To find what it is worth today you divide it by the growth factor for every year you have to wait.

PV = FV / (1 + r)N

FV is the payment. N is the number of years from today until the payment lands. r is the rate per year. The name of this move is discounting: taking a future payment back to what it is worth today.

A check dated years from now: zero-coupon bond price and rate · 1:42

Two things pull the value down, and they act on their own. A higher rate pulls it down. A longer wait pulls it down. Both together pull it down hard.

Count N as the gap between today and the payment date. A payment at the end of year 1 is divided by (1 + r) once. It is not left alone.

Worked example

You own Infosoft, a small software firm. You rent your office, and you owe the landlord one single payment of 500,000 ten years from now. The right discount rate for this payment is 10 percent. What is that payment worth today?
1
Name the shape.
One payment, one date. This is a single cash flow, so use PV = FV / (1 + r)N.
2
Take the inputs out of the question.
FV = 500,000   r = 0.10   N = 10
3
Build the growth factor first, on its own.
1.1010 = 2.5937
4
Divide the payment by that factor.
PV = 500,000 / 2.5937 = 192,771.64
About 192,772 today. Check it: 192,771.64 × 2.5937 gives back 500,000.
Try it. A government zero-coupon bond pays no interest along the way. It pays its face value of 100 in three years and nothing before that. Face value is the amount the borrower promised to pay back. The required return is 2 percent a year. What is the price of the bond today?
A zero-coupon bond is one single payment, so the price is just its present value.
1.023 = 1.061208, and 100 / 1.061208 = 94.23.
Check: 94.23 × 1.061208 = 100.00. A price under the face value is what you expect here, because the buyer gets nothing at all until year 3.
Try it. A zero-coupon bond pays 100 in six years. The required return falls from 8 percent to 5 percent. What happens to the price, and by how much?
At 8 percent: 100 / 1.086 = 100 / 1.586874 = 63.02.
At 5 percent: 100 / 1.056 = 100 / 1.340096 = 74.62.
The price rises by about 11.60. A smaller divider leaves a bigger number. Price and rate always move in opposite directions, and you will meet this again on day 6 with coupon bonds.

3. One move, two directions

Compounding and discounting: one move, two directions · 1:40

Compounding and discounting are not two topics. They are one move. Multiply by (1 + r)N to walk forward in time. Divide by the same factor to walk back.

FV = PV × (1 + r)N

The college fund: finding the rate, and the equal-slices trap · 1:43

Because it is one equation with four parts, any three of them give you the fourth. If you know both ends and the number of years, the rate falls out:

r = (FV / PV)1/N − 1

Notice the power of 1/N, not a division by N. That is because of interest on interest. In year 2 you earn a return on year 1's return as well as on your own money, so the years do not simply add up.

Going forward, multiply. Coming back, divide. Looking for the rate, take the Nth root of the two ends divided by each other, then take one away.

The same factor does both jobs. Only the direction of travel changes.

Worked example

You have just put 2,000 into a retirement account. You expect to earn 9 percent a year and you will never add another cent. You retire in 45 years. What will the account be worth then?
1
Name the direction.
You have money today and you want its value later, so you are walking forward. Use FV = PV × (1 + r)N.
2
Take the inputs out of the question.
PV = 2,000   r = 0.09   N = 45
3
Build the growth factor.
1.0945 = 48.3273
4
Multiply.
FV = 2,000 × 48.3273 = 96,654.57
About 96,655. Check it by walking back: 96,654.57 / 48.3273 = 2,000. Simple interest at the same 9 percent would have given only 2,000 × (1 + 0.09 × 45) = 10,100. Interest on interest is the whole difference.
Try it. College will cost 200,000 when your child starts in 18 years. You have 27,000 to invest today and you will add nothing. What annual rate do you need?
You know both ends and the years, so solve for r.
27,000 × (1 + r)18 = 200,000, so (1 + r)18 = 7.4074.
r = 7.40741/18 − 1 = 1.117673 − 1 = 11.77 percent a year.
Check: 27,000 × 1.11767318 = 200,000. Watch the trap: 7.4074 divided by 18 is not the answer, because the years compound.
Try it. A two-year zero-coupon bond with a face value of 100 is priced at 90 today. What is the required return per year, in annual effective terms? That means the true rate per year, once interest on interest is counted.
90 × (1 + r)2 = 100, so r = (100 / 90)1/2 − 1 = 5.41 percent.
Check: 90 × 1.054093 = 94.87, and 94.87 × 1.054093 = 100.
Your class file rounds this to 5.4 percent. Both are the same answer, just carried to a different number of digits.
The trap: the total return over the two years is 100 / 90 − 1 = 11.11 percent, and half of that is 5.56 percent, which is not the answer. Splitting a return in half ignores interest on interest.
