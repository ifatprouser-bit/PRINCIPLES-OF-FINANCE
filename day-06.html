Bonds, and the risks inside them

A bond is a loan with its payment dates printed on it. Read the dates, price the pieces, then ask what can go wrong.

Before you start: a guess, and today's words · 1:17
Five minutes: recall

Day 2 was the single cash flow. Answer both on paper before you open anything.

1. The course gives three reasons why money you receive later is worth less to you today. Name them.

2. You will receive 5,000 at the end of year 4. The rate is 7 percent. What is that worth today? And if you instead put 5,000 in the bank today at 7 percent, what do you hold at the end of year 4?

Write your two answers down, then check.

1. People would rather spend now than later, so they must be paid to wait. Inflation eats the buying power of money over time. And future cash flows are uncertain, so investors ask for more when the payment is less sure.

2. Value today, discounting: 5,000 / 1.074 = 5,000 / 1.310796 = 3,814.48

Value at the end of year 4, compounding: 5,000 × 1.074 = 6,553.98

Check: 3,814.48 × 1.074 = 5,000.00. Discounting and compounding are the same move in opposite directions. Today you will use both on one security.

1. Value the bond: zero coupon, coupon bond, perpetuity

What a bond is: a loan on paper you can sell · 1:40

What a debt security is

A debt security, also called a fixed income security, is a loan you can buy and sell. You hand money to the borrower today. The borrower promises you fixed payments later. Those payments are written in the contract before you buy. That is why this group is called fixed income.

From the buyer's side the cash moves twice. Cash goes out once, when you pay the price today. Cash comes in later, as coupons along the way and as the par value at the end.

WordWhat it means
Par valueThe amount the borrower repays at maturity. Also called face value, or the principal, which is the sum first borrowed. Usually 1,000 on a corporate or Treasury bond.
Coupon rateA percentage printed on the bond. It never changes. It exists only to fix the size of the payment.
Coupon paymentCoupon rate × par value. A 6 percent coupon rate on a par of 1,000 pays 60 every year.
MaturityThe date the par value comes back. The last year pays the coupon and the par together, so 60 + 1,000 = 1,060.
YieldThe return buyers ask for today to hold this bond. The course also calls it the required rate of return or the discount rate. All three are the same number.

Three shapes, and you already own all three

A bond's price: an annuity plus one single payment · 1:46

A bond is priced the same way as anything else: the present value of what it pays. Which formula you reach for depends only on the shape of the payments.

BondPaymentsTool
Zero couponNothing until maturity, then the par value onceSingle cash flow, day 2
Coupon bondThe same coupon every year, plus the par value at the endAnnuity plus a single cash flow, which is the mixed stream of day 5
Perpetuity bondThe same coupon every year forever, and the principal never comes backPerpetuity, day 4

So a coupon bond is not a new object. It is the day 5 lesson in new clothes: one annuity and one single cash flow, valued one at a time and then added. The professor prices it exactly that way in class.

Before you do any sums, say out loud which of the three shapes is in front of you. Zero coupon means one payment. Coupon bond means annuity plus one payment. Perpetuity means the principal never returns.
A 20 year coupon bond. Green arrows come in to you, the red arrow goes out. Years 1 to 20 are one annuity of 60. The extra 1,000 in year 20 is one single cash flow.

Worked example: the bond the professor prices in class

A plain coupon bond has a par value of 1,000 and matures in 20 years. It promises interest of 6 percent each year. Bonds of this risk yield 5 percent a year. What is the bond worth today?
1
Name the shape and the inputs. Coupon bond, so annuity plus single cash flow. Coupon payment = 0.06 × 1,000 = 60, paid at the end of years 1 to 20. Par value 1,000 arrives at the end of year 20. The discount rate is the yield, 5 percent, not the 6 percent.
2
Value the 20 coupons as an annuity. 60 × (1 − 1.05−20) / 0.05 = 60 × 12.462210 = 747.73
3
Value the par value as a single cash flow. 1,000 / 1.0520 = 1,000 / 2.653298 = 376.89
4
Add. Both pieces now sit at year 0, so adding is allowed. 747.73 + 376.89 = 1,124.62
The bond is worth 1,124.62 today. That is 124.62 above its par value of 1,000.

Sense check: buyers only need 5 percent, and this bond hands them 6 percent of 1,000 every year. They will pay more than 1,000 to get it. The answer being above par is the expected direction, not a surprise.

The perpetuity case

Some debt pays a coupon forever and never returns the principal. The course calls this a perpetuity, and the notes give the example of a consol, an old British bond with no end date. There is no par value to discount, because it never comes back. So the whole price is the perpetuity formula. A bond paying 50 a year forever, when buyers ask for 8 percent, is worth 50 / 0.08 = 625.00.

Try it. A zero coupon bond repays 1,000 at the end of year 6 and pays nothing before that. Buyers ask for 6 percent. What is it worth today?

One payment, so one single cash flow. No annuity anywhere.

1,000 / 1.066 = 1,000 / 1.418519 = 704.96

A zero coupon bond always trades below its par value. The only thing it gives you is one payment in the future, and the future is discounted. This is why the course says T-bills are sold at a discount.

Try it. A bond has a par value of 5,000, a coupon rate of 4.5 percent and 8 years to maturity. How much cash do you receive at the end of year 3, and how much at the end of year 8?

Coupon payment = 0.045 × 5,000 = 225. That arrives at the end of every year, including year 3.

Year 8 is the last year, so it carries the coupon and the par value together: 225 + 5,000 = 5,225.

Notice what the coupon rate did here. It sized the payment. It said nothing about what the bond is worth or what you earn. That job belongs to the yield.

2. Price against rates, and the coupon rate trap

Coupon rate or yield: which one do you discount with? · 1:46

Two percentages that look alike and do different jobs

This is the single place where marks are lost on bonds. Two numbers on the page are both percentages. They are not the same thing and they almost never have the same value.

Coupon rateYield
Who sets itThe borrower who sells the bond, once, on the day it is bornThe market, every day it trades
Does it changeNeverAll the time
What it doesFixes the size of the coupon paymentIs the rate you discount with
In the formulaSits on top, inside the paymentSits underneath, in (1 + r)

If you discount with the coupon rate, you will always get exactly the par value back, whatever the question said. That is the trap. Your answer will be a neat, round 1,000, and it will be wrong.

Premium, discount, and par

Yield up, price down: premium, discount and par · 1:46

Compare the two rates and the price follows. The professor puts it in one line in class: when the required rate of return is lower than the coupon rate, the bond trades in premium. When the coupon rate is smaller than the required rate of return, it trades in discount. When both are equal, it trades at par and the price is exactly 1,000.

TestNamePrice
Coupon rate above the yieldPremium bondAbove par
Coupon rate below the yieldDiscount bondBelow par
Coupon rate equal to the yieldTrading at parExactly par

The seesaw

Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions. Always. The reason is not a rule to learn by heart. It is the formula. The promised payments are fixed by contract, so when the discount rate under them rises, their present value has to fall.

The professor's own story: a government issues a bond with a 5 percent coupon at a time when buyers require 5 percent, so it trades at 1,000. Then people start to expect more inflation. They will no longer accept 5 percent. They still receive the same promised money, so the only way for them to earn more is to pay less. The price slides to, say, 900, and now the bond is a discount bond.

Rates up, price down. Rates down, price up. The coupon never moves, so the price has to do all the moving.
One bond, five years, coupon rate 6 percent, par 1,000. Only the yield changes along the bottom. The line never turns upward.

Worked example: a discount bond

A bond has a par value of 1,000 and 10 years left to maturity. Its coupon rate is 4 percent, paid once a year. Investors buying bonds of this risk now ask for 6 percent. What is the bond worth today?
1
Sort the two percentages before touching the calculator. 4 percent is the coupon rate, so it sizes the payment: 0.04 × 1,000 = 40 a year. 6 percent is the yield, so it is the discount rate. Coupon rate is below the yield, so expect a price below 1,000.
2
The 10 coupons of 40 are an annuity. 40 × (1 − 1.06−10) / 0.06 = 40 × 7.360087 = 294.40
3
The par value is a single cash flow at year 10. 1,000 / 1.0610 = 1,000 / 1.790848 = 558.39
4
Add the two pieces. 294.40 + 558.39 = 852.80
852.80. It is a discount bond, trading 147.20 below par.

Check the direction, not just the digits. You predicted below par in move 1 and you landed below par. If your answer had come out above 1,000, something went in the wrong slot.

Try it. One bond: par 1,000, coupon rate 6 percent, 5 years to maturity. Price it three times, at a yield of 4 percent, then 6 percent, then 8 percent. Name each result.

The coupon is 60 every year in all three cases. Only the discount rate changes.

At 4 percent: 60 × (1 − 1.04−5) / 0.04 + 1,000 / 1.045 = 1,089.04 Premium bond.

At 6 percent: 60 × (1 − 1.06−5) / 0.06 + 1,000 / 1.065 = 1,000.00 Trading at par, exactly. Coupon rate equals yield.

At 8 percent: 60 × (1 − 1.08−5) / 0.08 + 1,000 / 1.085 = 920.15 Discount bond.

Three prices, one bond, one direction. Keep the middle line in your head. When the two rates are equal the price is exactly par, and you do not need a calculator to say so.

3. The government ladder: bills, notes, bonds

T-bills pay no coupon: the interest is in the gap · 1:48

The United States Treasury is the borrower here, not the Federal Reserve. It borrows at three lengths, and the length decides the shape of the security.

NameMaturityShape
T-billUp to 1 yearZero coupon. Pays no coupon at all. Sold below 100 and paid back at 100.
T-note1 to 10 yearsCoupon bond. Pays a coupon and repays the par value at the end.
T-bondMore than 10 years, usually 20 or 30Coupon bond, same shape as a T-note, longer wait.
TIPSLong datedThe coupon and the principal are linked to inflation, so inflation risk is removed.

The Israeli version of the T-bill is the makam. Same idea: short, zero coupon, sold below its face value.

Where a T-bill's interest actually comes from

A T-bill pays no coupon, so at first it looks like it pays no interest. It does. You buy it below 100 and you are repaid 100. The gap is the interest. That is the whole idea.

What moves the yield of a T-bill

The three month bill is used as the standard for the risk free rate. The Treasury has never failed to pay, and it can make the currency its own debt is written in. So default, which means failure to pay, is not what moves this yield. Three things do.

What moves itHow it works
Expected inflationThe main driver in the course. Buyers price what they expect prices to do over the next months, not what inflation already was. When expected inflation rose in 2022 and 2023, short rates rose with it.
The real interest rateThe payment people demand simply for waiting, before inflation is counted at all.
Daily tradingBills change hands every day in the secondary market, where bonds that were sold long ago are traded again. The price is set there, and the yield is read back out of the price, so it can move day to day.
A T-bill's yield is not printed anywhere. It is worked backwards out of the price the market paid. Price and yield are two views of the same trade.

Worked example: reading the yield out of the price

A one year T-bill will pay 100 at maturity. It trades today at 95.60. What yield is the buyer earning? And if worries about inflation push buyers to demand 6 percent instead, what happens to the price?
1
Name the shape. A T-bill pays once, at maturity. It is a zero coupon bond, so it is a single cash flow. Price today 95.60, payment 100 one year out.
2
Turn the price into a rate. Price × (1 + r) = 100, so r = (100 / 95.60) − 1. 100 / 95.60 = 1.046025, so r = 4.60 percent
3
Read it in money. You put in 95.60 and get back 100. The gain is 4.40, and 4.40 on 95.60 is the same 4.60 percent. No coupon was ever paid.
4
Now push the required yield up to 6 percent. The promise of 100 does not change. Only the discount does. 100 / 1.06 = 94.34
The yield today is 4.60 percent. If buyers start demanding 6 percent, the price falls from 95.60 to 94.34, a fall of 1.26. Higher yield, lower price, on a bill just as on a bond.
Try it. A one year bill promises 100 at maturity. What do you pay if buyers ask for 3.5 percent? What if they ask for 5 percent? Which of T-bill, T-note and T-bond pay coupons?

At 3.5 percent: 100 / 1.035 = 96.62

At 5 percent: 100 / 1.05 = 95.24

Both are below 100, which is what a zero coupon security always does. The higher required yield gives the lower price, the seesaw again.

T-notes and T-bonds pay coupons. T-bills do not.

4. The risks inside a bond, and the bank case

Two risks: the money doesn't come, or it buys less · 1:52

Risk free debt against risky debt

In this course, risk free debt means government debt issued in the government's own currency. The state can tax, and it can create the currency the debt is written in, so failure to pay is close to impossible. That is why the short Treasury bill is used as the standard risk free rate.

Risky debt is everything where the promised money might not arrive. A company has no printing press. If its assets are worth less than what it owes, the payment is simply not there. The course notes are clear about the limit of the word. Risk free means free of default risk, not free of every risk. Inflation and currency risk stay.

The two risks the exam guide names

Inflation riskCredit risk
The worryThe money arrives in full but buys less than you hopedThe money does not arrive in full, or at all
Also calledPurchasing power riskDefault risk
Who has itAlmost every bond, government ones included. TIPS are the course's exceptionCorporate borrowers, and governments borrowing in a currency they cannot print
Where you see itIn the gap between the nominal rate, the one printed on the bond, and the real rate, the one left after inflationIn the yield spread over a government bond of the same length
Same promise, riskier borrower: why it pays a higher yield · 1:28

So corporate against government is not a question of who promises more. It is a question of who is more likely to keep the promise. Two bonds can promise the very same cash and trade at different prices, because buyers discount the shakier promise harder. The extra yield the corporate has to offer is what the course calls the credit spread, or yield spread, and it is a measure of risk.

Same promise, different borrower, different price. The riskier bond is always the cheaper one, because cheap is how the market pays you for carrying the risk.

Worked example: what credit risk costs

Two bonds each promise a single payment of 1,000 in one year and nothing before it. One is issued by the government, and buyers ask for 4 percent. The other is issued by a company, and buyers ask for 7 percent from that borrower. Price both. If inflation over the year turns out to be 3 percent, what does the government bond really earn?
1
Both are zero coupon, so both are single cash flows. Only the discount rate differs.
2
The government bond. 1,000 / 1.04 = 961.54
3
The company bond. 1,000 / 1.07 = 934.58
4
Read the difference. The company bond costs 26.96 less for the same promise. The credit spread is 7 − 4 = 3 percentage points, and 26.96 is what those 3 points are worth in cash today.
5
Now the inflation part. The course uses a close enough rule: real = nominal − inflation. 4 − 3 = 1 percent real The government bond removed credit risk. It did not remove inflation risk.
Government bond 961.54, company bond 934.58. The gap of 26.96 is the price of credit risk. The 3 percent inflation then takes most of what the safe bond earned. That is inflation risk at work on a bond with no default risk at all.

Commercial banks: deposits, subordinated debt, deposit insurance

The bank's line: depositors, subordinated debt, shareholders · 1:48

A commercial bank is a borrower like any other, and the course uses it as the clearest case of a debt ladder. It borrows from the public in the form of deposits, and it lends that money out. Deposits are its main debt. On top of deposits many banks also issue subordinated debt. That is the same thing as junior debt. It is paid only after the bank's other debt has been paid in full. Then come the shareholders, who are paid last and only from what is left.

Deposit insurance is a government promise to pay insured depositors back if the bank fails. It does not make the bank safe. It moves the depositor's risk onto the state. The holder of subordinated debt gets no such promise. That is exactly why subordinated debt has to offer a higher return.

The course files list deposit insurance as an exam topic. They give no coverage limit and no scheme name, so do not try to remember a number for it.

Try it. A small bank fails. It owes 100 to insured depositors and 20 to holders of its subordinated debt. Everything it owns is sold and raises 105. Who receives what, and who carries the loss?

Pay in order, never out of order.

Depositors are first in line and are owed 100. There is 105, so they are paid in full: 100. Their insurance is not even needed here.

Subordinated debt is next, owed 20, and only 105 − 100 = 5 is left: 5. They lose 15.

Shareholders are last and nothing remains: 0.

Check: 100 + 5 + 0 = 105, exactly what the assets raised. The pieces always add back to the assets. Day 7 turns this line of claims into the formulas you will use on the exam.
