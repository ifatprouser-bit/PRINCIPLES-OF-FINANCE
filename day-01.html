What kind of thing is this number

Before you work out any numbers, decide what you are looking at. A thing, or a claim on a thing. A size, or a move. And how much you really know when you choose.

Before you start: today's words, and one question to hold · 1:11
Five minutes: recall

This is the first day, so there is nothing to recall yet. Go straight to the guess below.

Guess first

A firm owns assets worth $700,000. It owes $450,000. Today the board pays $70,000 in cash to the shareholders. Nothing else changes.

Straight after the payment, are the shareholders richer, poorer, or exactly as rich as before?

Write your guess and one line of why. Do not work it out. This is a guess, not a test. You will check it in panel 3.

1. A thing, or a claim on a thing

The oven and the loan: does it produce, or is it a promise? · 1:47

The course splits every asset into two kinds.

Real assets set how much the economy can make, and how much income it earns. Land, machines, buildings. They make things.

Financial assets are claims on real assets. Shares and bonds. They do not add directly to what the economy can produce. A share is a claim on what a firm owns after its debts. A bond is a claim on a borrower. A bank deposit is a claim on the bank.

A claim always has two sides. It is an asset for the person who holds it, and a debt for the person who owes it. A machine has no other side. It just sits there and works.

Ask one question: does it produce, or is it a promise from someone else? Produces = real. Promise = financial.

Burn the top row and the economy can produce less. Tear up the bottom row and the machines still run. Only the owner of the claim has changed.

Worked example

A small shop chain owns its shop buildings, worth $400,000, and its delivery scooters and fridges, worth $100,000. It has borrowed $300,000 from a bank. The owners hold all the shares.

What are the real assets worth, and what is the owners' claim worth?
1
Sort each number.
Buildings and scooters and fridges are things that produce. They are real assets. The bank loan is a claim, so it is a financial asset for the bank and a debt for the shop.
2
Add the real assets.
400,000 + 100,000 = 500,000
3
Take out the claim that comes first.
The bank is owed $300,000. Whatever is left belongs to the owners.
500,000 − 300,000 = 200,000
4
Check that the claims add back up.
300,000 + 200,000 = 500,000
The two claims together are worth exactly the real assets they sit on. That is always true, and it comes back on day 7.
Real assets $500,000. The owners' claim is worth $200,000.
Try it. Sort these five into real and financial: a tractor, a wheat field, 50 shares in a food company, a loan agreement held by the bank that lent the money, a grain silo.
Real: the tractor, the wheat field, the grain silo. All three produce.
Financial: the 50 shares, and the loan agreement. Both are claims on someone else.
The loan is the one people get wrong. To the farmer it is a debt. To the bank that holds it, it is an asset, and a financial one.

2. Where the money goes, and where it only changes hands

The resold concert ticket: primary and secondary market · 1:49

Three kinds of player take part: households, corporations (companies) and government. Each one can be on either side. A household saves and also borrows. A government builds roads and also issues bonds.

Money flows in a loop. Investors hand cash to the firm. The firm buys real assets. The real assets throw off cash. The firm then chooses: keep the cash and put it back to work, or pay it out to investors.

There are two places where shares and bonds change hands, and they are not the same place.

MarketWhat happensDoes the firm get cash?
PrimaryThe firm sells new shares or new bonds. An IPO is the first time a firm sells shares to the public.Yes. This is the only way.
SecondaryOne investor sells to another investor, on an exchange such as the NYSE or NASDAQ.No. Nothing at all.
New paper sold by the firm = primary, and the cash goes into the firm. Old paper changing owner = secondary, and the firm is not in the room.

The loop is the whole investment process on one picture. The secondary market sits outside it: it changes who holds the claim, and nothing else.

Worked example

A firm sells 2,000,000 new shares at $18 each in its first public offering. Ignore all fees.
The next week, Dana buys 100 of those shares from Ron on the exchange at $20 each.

How much cash reaches the firm from each of the two events?
1
Name each event.
The firm is selling paper it has just created, so the offering is a primary market event. Dana and Ron are trading paper that already exists, so that is a secondary market event.
2
Cash from the offering.
2,000,000 × 18 = 36,000,000
3
Cash from the trade between Dana and Ron.
100 × 20 = 2,000
That $2,000 goes from Dana to Ron. The firm is not part of the deal, so the firm receives $0.
The firm receives $36,000,000 from the offering, and $0 from the trade between Dana and Ron.
Try it. A firm issues 300,000 new shares at $25 each. Ignore fees. How much new money reaches the firm? A month later, 50,000 of those shares change hands between two funds. Which market is each of the two events?
300,000 × 25 = $7,500,000 reaches the firm.
The issue is the primary market, because the shares are new and the firm is the seller.
The trade between the two funds is the secondary market. The firm receives nothing from it.

3. Equity is a size. A dividend is a move.

The cash machine and the bakery cash box: equity and dividend · 1:42

This is the confusion that costs the most marks, so read it slowly.

Equity is what the shareholders' claim is worth. It is pure leftover: the value of the assets, minus what the debt holders are owed. Nobody sets it. Nobody votes on it. It is whatever is left.

A dividend is cash moving out of the firm and into the shareholder's pocket. Somebody does decide it: the board. It changes where the value sits. It does not change how much value there is.

Think of a bank account. The balance is the value. A withdrawal is the dividend. Taking $100 out of your account does not make you $100 richer. The money is in your hand instead of in the account, and your total is the same.

So when the firm pays a dividend, two things move together. The firm's assets fall by the cash that left, which pulls the equity down. The shareholder's pocket rises by the same cash. Add the two and the shareholder is exactly where they started.

Before you answer, ask: is this question about how big, or about how much moved? Equity, value, worth = a size. Dividend, payment, payout = a move.

The purple block shrinks and the amber block appears. The two together never change. That is what a move looks like.

Worked example

A firm's assets are worth $600,000. It owes debt with face value $250,000. Face value is the amount the firm promised to pay back. Today the board pays a cash dividend of $80,000. Nothing else changes that day.

What is the equity worth before, what is it worth after, and are the shareholders better off?
1
Equity before. Assets minus what the debt holders are owed.
600,000 − 250,000 = 350,000
2
The cash leaves the firm, so the assets fall.
600,000 − 80,000 = 520,000
3
The debt does not change, so work out the leftover again.
520,000 − 250,000 = 270,000
4
Add what the shareholders now hold in their hand.
270,000 + 80,000 = 350,000
Same as line 1. The dividend moved value from inside the firm to outside it.
Equity falls from $350,000 to $270,000. Shareholder wealth stays at $350,000. The dividend is a move, not a gain.
Try it. A firm's assets are worth $900,000 and it owes senior debt with face value $350,000. The firm uses $150,000 of cash to repay part of that debt. Nothing else changes. What is the equity worth now, and why is the answer different in kind from the dividend case above?
Equity before: 900,000 − 350,000 = 550,000.
Assets after the repayment: 900,000 − 150,000 = 750,000.
Debt after: 350,000 − 150,000 = 200,000.
Equity after: 750,000 − 200,000 = 550,000. Unchanged.
Both sides fell by the same $150,000, so the leftover is the same. A dividend takes cash out and gives nothing back to the firm, so equity falls. A debt repayment takes cash out and cancels an equal claim, so equity stands still. Both are moves. Neither creates value.
Try it, the other way round. After a cash dividend, a firm's equity is worth $220,000. The shareholders also hold the dividend cash, and their total wealth is unchanged at $280,000. How big was the dividend?
Wealth after = equity after + cash in the pocket, and wealth did not change.
280,000 − 220,000 = 60,000.
The dividend was $60,000. When you are given the total and one part, the move is just the gap.

4. The tasks, the form of the firm, and whose decision it is

Everything a finance manager does falls into one of three tasks. The names matter because exam questions often just ask which task a story is about.

TaskThe question it answers
Investment, or capital budgetingWhich real assets should we own?
FinancingWhere does the money come from: shares, senior debt (paid back first), junior debt (paid back after)?
PayoutCash has come back. Put it back to work, or pay it out?
The three shapes of a business, and what only a corporation gives · 1:42

Before any of that, somebody chose what kind of firm to build. The course sets three forms against each other.

FormWhat you getWhat it costs you
Sole proprietorshipA one-person business. Easy to start. The profit is taxed once and it is all yours.If the firm cannot pay a debt, they come after your own money. The firm ends with you. Raising money is hard.
PartnershipMore owners, so more money to start with. The profit is still taxed once.General partners must pay the firm's debts out of their own money. A stake is hard to pass on. The firm breaks up easily.
CorporationLimited liability: if the firm cannot pay, your own money is safe. The firm can live on after its owners. A stake changes hands without ending the firm, so money can be raised from strangers.The profit is taxed twice. Owners and managers are no longer the same people.
Taxed twice, and how it shows up in a question · 1:33

Taxed twice means this. The company pays tax on its profit. What is left can go out as a dividend, and the shareholder pays tax on that dividend too. The same money is taxed leaving the firm and again entering the pocket. The owner of a one-person business, and a partner, pay tax once.

Limited liability, a life longer than the owners, and shares a stranger can buy: only the corporation gives all three. The bill for them is double taxation.

One more label sits on top of every story: whose money and whose choice it is.

LabelWho is deciding
PersonalA household, about its own money. Borrow, save, invest.
CorporateA firm, about the firm's money. Which assets, how to pay for them, what to pay out.
PublicA government or a city, about public money. Tax, issue bonds, pay subsidies: money that helps an industry.

A story can carry two labels when two kinds of decision maker each make a real choice. In this course's own answer keys, a private bank simply lending you money does not make a case corporate, because the bank is only the other side of the deal. A government subsidy or a tax rule does make it public.

Two questions, in this order. Which task: buy an asset, raise money, or pay cash out? Then: who is actually choosing, a household, a firm, a government, or two of them?

Worked example

Three friends are starting a coffee chain. They want to open 4 branches at $250,000 each, paid for by a bank loan or by new shares. They also want to sell a stake to outsiders later, with none of them personally liable.

How much is at stake, which tasks are these, which form fits, and which label?
1
Size the decision.
4 × 250,000 = 1,000,000
2
Sort the two choices into tasks.
Branches are more real assets: capital budgeting. Loan or new shares is where the money comes from: the financing decision.
3
Match the form to what they asked for.
A sole proprietorship has one owner, who must pay for everything. A partnership leaves the general partners liable and a stake hard to pass on. Only the corporation gives both of the things they asked for.
4
Name the price, and put the label on.
Their profit will be taxed at the company and again as a dividend in each friend's hands. As a partnership it would be taxed once. A firm is deciding about the firm's money, so the label is corporate finance.
$1,000,000 at stake. Capital budgeting, then financing. Form: a corporation, paid for with double taxation. Label: corporate finance.
Try it. (a) A hospital owned by a private company decides whether to buy a new scanner. Which task, and which label? (b) The same scanner, but a government ministry buys it for a state hospital. What changes? (c) A woman opens a small repair shop with her own savings and wants no company paperwork. Which form, and what does she give up?
(a) Capital budgeting: which real asset to own. Corporate finance, because a firm is deciding about the firm's money.
(b) The task does not change. Only the label does, to public finance, because a government is now spending public money.
(c) A sole proprietorship. Taxed once, hers to keep, open tomorrow. She gives up limited liability, so her own savings stand behind every debt of the shop, and the business ends when she stops.

5. What you know when you decide

Three shops: certainty, uncertainty, ambiguity · 1:29

One more sorting question, and it changes which tools you are allowed to use. How much do you actually know at the moment you choose?

The course quiz uses three labels: certainty, uncertainty and ambiguity. The slides give the labels and the answer for each case, but they never write out a definition. The three meanings below are the standard split used in finance. They fit the way the course answers mark each case. If the professor words it differently in class, his wording wins.

EnvironmentWhat you knowA small example
CertaintyThe amount is fixed before the event happens.A bakery has already sold 1,000 cakes for next month at $12 each, paid in advance. The $12,000 is settled.
UncertaintyThe outcome is not known, but you can put odds on it that you can trust, from real data or a tested model.A taxi firm has five years of its own daily sales. From them: a good day 60 percent of the time, an average day 30 percent, a bad day 10 percent.
AmbiguityThe outcome is not known, and nobody can work out odds you could trust.A farmer is offered a contract to grow a crop that has never been grown in that soil, in a season for which nobody holds records.
Two questions, in this order. Is the amount fixed before the event? If yes, certainty. If no, can anyone put odds you could trust on the outcomes? If yes, uncertainty. If no, ambiguity.
The hotel pool: half fixed, half open · 1:11

Most real decisions hold a fixed part and an open part. The label follows the part you cannot pin down. A price you have locked, sitting next to a quantity nobody can name, is still not certainty.

The bars are the chance of each outcome. In the middle you can draw them. On the right you cannot, and that is the whole difference between uncertainty and ambiguity.

Worked example

A hotel is deciding whether to heat its pool this winter. The gas for the whole winter is already fixed by a signed contract at $8,000. Guest numbers are not fixed, but the hotel holds its own guest records for the last eight winters, and they show a clear pattern.

Which environment is this decision in?
1
Split the decision into its parts.
The cost side is the gas. The benefit side is the guests who use the pool.
2
Test the cost side.
The $8,000 is fixed by contract before the winter starts. On its own, that part is certainty.
3
Test the benefit side.
Guest numbers are not known. But eight winters of the hotel's own records let somebody write down odds you can trust. That is uncertainty, not ambiguity.
4
Take the label from the part you cannot pin down.
One part is fixed and one part is open, so the decision as a whole is not certain.
Uncertainty. The fixed $8,000 does not make the decision certain, because the side that decides the answer is still open.
Try it. (a) A farmer is offered $40,000 today for his whole harvest, to be paid whatever the weather does. (b) An insurer is asked to cover a brand new kind of machine that has never been built before, so nobody holds any record of how often such a machine breaks. Which environment is each one?
(a) Certainty. The amount the farmer receives is fixed before the harvest. The weather is still open, but it no longer touches his money, because he sold the risk with the crop.
(b) Ambiguity. The outcome is open and there is no record to build odds from. Uncertainty would need a source of odds you can trust, and a machine that has never existed has none.
