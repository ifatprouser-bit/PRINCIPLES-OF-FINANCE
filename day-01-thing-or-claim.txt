NOA: Picture the place where you buy bread. In the back there's an oven. In a bank somewhere there's a loan agreement, because the oven was bought with borrowed money. If one of those disappeared tonight, which one stops the bread tomorrow?

NOA: Take a moment. Choose before I say it.

TOM: The oven. If the agreement burned, bread still comes out in the morning. Someone would just be arguing about who owes who.

NOA: You found it in one sentence. The oven is a real asset. It produces. The loan is a financial asset. It's a promise from someone else.

TOM: And the loan has two sides. To the bakery a debt, to the bank an asset.

NOA: Same paper, opposite signs. The oven has no other side. It stands there and works.

TOM: Let me move it. A small workshop that sews. The machines are real, they make clothes. The founder's sister holds shares in it.

NOA: So what does the sister own?

TOM: Not a machine. Whatever's left after everyone the workshop owes has been paid. A claim on the leftover.

NOA: Remember that sentence. You need it within the hour.

TOM: One more. My bank balance isn't money in a drawer. It's the bank's promise to pay me. A claim.

NOA: And the delivery van of the shop next door is real. Here's the test that settles every case. Burn all the real assets and the economy produces less tomorrow. Tear up all the claims and every machine still runs. Only the owner changed.

TOM: So claims are not useless. They move who owns things, instead of adding to the pile.

NOA: Take a breath and recall one thing. One question sorts every asset you will meet. Does it produce, or is it a promise from someone else?
