Day 9 · Sunday 4 October

Projects: should we do this at all

A project is just a stream of cash flows with a price tag on the front. Today you learn how to say yes or no to it.

Before you start: today's words · 1:14
Five minutes: recall

Day 6 was bonds. Answer both on paper before you open anything.

1. A bond has a par value of 1,000 and a coupon rate of 6 percent, paid once a year. What exactly is the par value, and how much cash does the coupon pay each year?

2. That bond has 3 years left. Market rates for bonds of this risk are now 8 percent. Is the price above 1,000, below 1,000, or exactly 1,000? Work out the price, and name that kind of bond.

Write your two answers down, then check.

1. The par value, also called the face value, is the fixed amount the borrower pays back on the last day. It is not the price. The coupon rate is applied to the par value, not to the price, so the cash coupon is 0.06 × 1,000 = 60 every year.

2. Below 1,000. The coupon is fixed at 60 forever, so when buyers can get 8 percent elsewhere, the only way this bond can compete is a lower price. That is the seesaw: rates up, price down.

Price = the three coupons as an annuity, plus the par value as a single cash flow:

60 × (1 − 1.08−3) / 0.08 = 60 × 2.577097 = 154.63

1,000 / 1.083 = 1,000 / 1.259712 = 793.83

154.63 + 793.83 = 948.46

948.46 is below par, so this is a discount bond. The coupon rate of 6 percent is below the yield of 8 percent, and that is always the sign.

Guess first

Two projects. You can only do one of them. Your required return is 10 percent.

Project small: pay 1,000 today, receive 1,300 at the end of one year. That is a 30 percent return.

Project big: pay 100,000 today, receive 120,000 at the end of one year. That is a 20 percent return.

Which one makes the owners richer?

Guess now. No answer is shown here. This is a guess, not a test. The whole page answers it.

1. Net present value: what the project is worth, minus what it costs

The washing machine: is it worth more than it costs? · 1:47

Every project you will meet on this exam has the same shape. You pay something today. Money comes back later. The question is always the same: is the money that comes back worth more, seen from today, than the money you pay out today.

You already know how to answer that. Day 2 taught you that cash from different years cannot be added until you bring it all to one date. So bring every future inflow back to year 0, add them, and compare the total with the cost. The gap between those two numbers is the net present value, or NPV.

The professor's own guide for the final describes NPV in four words: "excess of market value over cost". In plain words, that is how much more the future cash is worth today than what you pay for it. Market value means what the future cash is worth today. Cost means what you hand over today. NPV is what is left over. It is money the owners gain the moment the project is taken.

On the formula sheet it looks like this:

NPV = Σ CFi / (1+r)i − I0

CFi is the cash flow in year i. r is the required rate of return, the return you could get elsewhere at the same risk. I0 is the cost of the initial investment, which is the money you pay at year 0. Notice there is no new formula here. The first part is the present value of a stream of cash flows, which you have used since day 5. The only new thing is taking the price away at the end.

The green piece is the whole point. It is the value the project adds on top of paying for itself. If the top bar were shorter than the bottom bar there would be no green piece, and the project would destroy value.

Take the project if NPV is above zero. Reject it if NPV is below zero. At exactly zero the project earns exactly the required return and adds nothing, so you are free either way.

Why that rule and not something else? A positive NPV means the future cash is worth more than the asking price, once you price it at the going rate for that level of risk. You could sell the project on the day you bought it and pocket the difference. A negative NPV means you could get the same future cash more cheaply somewhere else at the same risk.

Worked example

This is the shape the professor names in his guide for the final, so learn it in this form.

A project needs an initial investment of 50,000 at year 0. It brings in 20,000 at the end of year 1, 25,000 at the end of year 2 and 30,000 at the end of year 3. The discount rate is 10 percent. What is the NPV of the project, and should the firm take it?
1
Sort the numbers before touching the calculator.
I0 = 50,000, paid at year 0. Three inflows, all different sizes, so this is not an annuity. It is three single cash flows. r = 0.10.
2
Bring each inflow back to year 0 on its own.
20,000 / 1.10 = 18,181.82
25,000 / 1.21 = 20,661.16
30,000 / 1.331 = 22,539.44
3
Now they all sit on the same date, so add them.
18,181.82 + 20,661.16 + 22,539.44 = 61,382.42
4
Take away what you pay today.
NPV = 61,382.42 − 50,000 = 11,382.42
5
Check the size before you write it down.
Total cash coming in is 75,000. Discounting must shrink it, and 61,382.42 is smaller than 75,000. Good. The NPV must then be smaller than 75,000 − 50,000 = 25,000, and 11,382.42 is.
NPV = 11,382.42. It is above zero, so take the project.

One line about the payback period

The syllabus lists a third method, the payback period. It counts how many years pass before the cash coming in adds up to the cost. Here 20,000 plus 25,000 is 45,000 by the end of year 2, and the last 5,000 arrives during year 3, so the payback is about 2.2 years. It is quick, and it is weak. It adds cash from different years without discounting, and it ignores everything that happens after the cost is covered. The class material in this pack does not take it further, so do not expect a deep question on it.

Try it. A project costs 40,000 at year 0. It brings in 15,000 at the end of year 1, 18,000 at the end of year 2 and 12,000 at the end of year 3.
(a) At a discount rate of 12 percent, what is the NPV, and what is your decision?
(b) The required return then drops to 4 percent. Redo it. Does the decision change?

(a) At 12 percent.

15,000 / 1.12 = 13,392.86

18,000 / 1.2544 = 14,349.49

12,000 / 1.404928 = 8,541.36

13,392.86 + 14,349.49 + 8,541.36 = 36,283.71

NPV = 36,283.71 − 40,000 = −3,716.29. Below zero, so reject.

(b) At 4 percent.

15,000 / 1.04 = 14,423.08

18,000 / 1.0816 = 16,642.01

12,000 / 1.124864 = 10,667.96

NPV = 41,733.05 − 40,000 = 1,733.05. Above zero, so accept.

Yes, the decision changes. Nothing about the project changed. Only the price of waiting changed. A lower discount rate makes far-away money worth more today, so NPV rises. This project is worth doing only when money is cheap.

2. Internal rate of return, and the four ways it lets you down

IRR: the highest loan rate where the project still breaks even · 1:49

NPV answers in money. Many people would rather hear a percent. A percent can be set beside the rate on a loan or the return on a share without knowing the size of anything. That percent is the internal rate of return, or IRR.

The definition is short. The IRR is the discount rate that makes the NPV of the project exactly zero. It is the rate at which the project just breaks even. Nothing is looked up and nothing is given to you. The cash flows themselves decide it, which is what "internal" means.

Reading it is easy. If the IRR is above your required return, the project earns more than you could get elsewhere, so NPV at your required return must be positive. If the IRR is below your required return, NPV must be negative. Those two rules are the same rule seen from two sides, as long as the project is normal: money out first, then money in, and never out again.

The line is the same project priced at many different rates. It slopes down because a higher rate shrinks future money. Where it cuts the axis, NPV is zero, and that rate is the IRR. Anywhere left of that point the NPV is positive.

Take the project if IRR is above the required return r. This gives the same answer as NPV for a single normal project. For anything else, trust NPV.

Worked example

A machine costs 8,000 today. It is sold at the end of year 2 and returns a single amount of 9,680. Nothing happens in between. The required return is 7 percent. Find the IRR and decide.
1
Write down what "NPV is zero" means here.
−8,000 + 9,680 / (1 + IRR)2 = 0
So 8,000 must grow into 9,680 in two years at the rate IRR.
2
Divide, to see the total growth over the two years.
9,680 / 8,000 = 1.21
3
Take the root, because the growth happened over two years, not one.
1.211/2 = 1.10, so IRR = 0.10 = 10 percent
4
Decide, then confirm with NPV.
10 percent is above the required 7 percent, so accept.
NPV = 9,680 / 1.1449 − 8,000 = 8,454.89 − 8,000 = 454.89
Positive, as the IRR promised. The two rules agree.
5
Plug the answer back in.
8,000 × 1.102 = 8,000 × 1.21 = 9,680
Exactly the cash flow in the question, so 10 percent is right.
IRR = 10 percent, above the required 7 percent, so accept. NPV at 7 percent is 454.89.

That example was kind: two cash flows, so one clean answer. With three or more inflows there is no formula. You guess a rate, work out the NPV, and move your guess until the NPV changes sign. In the exam you are normally given a few rates in the options, so test those instead of guessing blind.

The problems with IRR

When IRR lets you down: two answers, borrowing, reinvesting · 1:48

This is a named topic on the syllabus, so expect it. IRR is a summary, and every summary throws something away.

The problemWhat goes wrong
RankingWhen you must pick one project out of two, the higher IRR is not always the better choice. IRR is blind to size and to timing. Panel 3 shows this happening.
More than one answerIf the cash flows change sign more than once, more than one rate can make NPV zero. With two answers, the rule "is IRR above r" has no meaning.
Borrowing, not lendingIf money comes in first and goes out later, the rule turns upside down. You then want a low IRR, because you are the borrower.
Putting the money back to workIRR quietly assumes every inflow is put back to work at the IRR itself. A 40 percent IRR rarely means you can put that money back to work at 40 percent.

Here is the second problem with real numbers. A project pays out 1,000 today, brings in 2,600 at the end of year 1, then needs 1,650 spent at the end of year 2 to close it down. The signs go minus, plus, minus. Two changes of sign, so look for two answers:

Discount rate0%10%20%30%50%60%
NPV−50.000.0020.8323.670.00−19.53

NPV is zero at 10 percent and zero again at 50 percent. Both are the IRR. Asking whether "the IRR" beats your required return has no answer here, because there are two of them. The way out is to ignore IRR and read the NPV at the rate that actually applies to you.

Try it. A project costs 5,000 today and returns 1,500 at the end of each of the next 5 years. Compute the NPV at 15 percent and at 16 percent. Between which two rates does the IRR sit, and is it nearer to one end than the other?

Five equal payments in a row, so this is an annuity. Use the present value of an annuity factor.

At 15 percent: (1 − 1.15−5) / 0.15 = 3.352155

1,500 × 3.352155 = 5,028.23, so NPV = 28.23

At 16 percent: (1 − 1.16−5) / 0.16 = 3.274294

1,500 × 3.274294 = 4,911.44, so NPV = −88.56

NPV is positive at 15 percent and negative at 16 percent, so the IRR sits between them. It is much nearer to 15 percent, because 28.23 is a much smaller distance from zero than 88.56. The true value is 15.24 percent.

This is the whole method for a project with many inflows. Find two rates where the sign changes, then close in from both sides.

3. Independent or mutually exclusive: the question that changes the rule

Can you take both, or must you pick one? · 1:34

Before you compare two projects you have to know what kind of pair they are. The syllabus names both kinds, and the exam guide names them too.

Kind of pairWhat it means and what you do
IndependentTaking one does not stop you taking the other. Judge each project on its own. Take every one with NPV above zero. There is no ranking to do.
Mutually exclusiveOnly one can be taken. One plot of land, one machine slot, one team. Now you must put them in order, and you do that by NPV. The winner is the one with the highest NPV at your required return, as long as that NPV is above zero.
Size: a better rate is not more money · 1:27

Putting projects in order is where IRR breaks. A percent tells you how hard each dollar works. It does not tell you how many dollars there are, or for how long they work. Two things can pull the order apart.

Size. A tiny project can earn a huge percent and still add almost nothing. That is the pretest at the top of this page. Thirty percent on 1,000 is a gain of 181.82 in present value. Twenty percent on 100,000 is a gain of 9,090.91. The smaller percent wins by a distance, because the firm banks money, not percentages.

Timing: paid soon or paid later, and where the lines cross · 1:52

Timing. A project that pays back fast has a high IRR because its cash does not sit and wait. A project that pays later can still be worth more in total. Which of the two wins depends on the discount rate, and that is what the next example shows.

Independent projects: take every project with NPV above zero. Mutually exclusive projects: take the single highest NPV. When NPV and IRR disagree, NPV is the answer, because NPV is measured in money.

Worked example

Two projects, and the firm can take only one of them. Both cost 10,000 at year 0. Project S returns 6,500 at the end of year 1 and 6,500 at the end of year 2, and then stops. Project L returns nothing in years 1 and 2, then 15,000 at the end of year 3. The required return is 8 percent. Which project should the firm take?
1
NPV of S at 8 percent.
6,500 / 1.08 = 6,018.52
6,500 / 1.1664 = 5,572.70
NPV = 6,018.52 + 5,572.70 − 10,000 = 1,591.22
2
NPV of L at 8 percent.
15,000 / 1.259712 = 11,907.48
NPV = 11,907.48 − 10,000 = 1,907.48
3
Now the IRR of each, for comparison.
S: the rate where 6,500 twice exactly repays 10,000. Trial and error gives 19.43 percent.
L: 15,000 / 10,000 = 1.5, then 1.51/3 = 1.144714, so IRR = 14.47 percent
4
Line the two rules up side by side.
ProjectNPV at 8%IRR
S1,591.2219.43%
L1,907.4814.47%
NPV points at L. IRR points at S. They disagree.
5
Follow NPV, and say why.
L adds 1,907.48 − 1,591.22 = 316.26 more to the value of the firm today. S has the better percent because its cash comes back sooner, but there is less of it. The owners spend money, not percent.
Take project L. Its NPV of 1,907.48 is the highest, even though project S has the higher IRR.

L is steeper because all of its cash sits at year 3, and far-away money suffers most when the rate rises. Left of the crossing point L wins. Right of it S wins. The rate you are given decides which side of the picture you are standing on.

Try it. Keep the same two projects, S and L, with the same cash flows. The required return is now 16 percent instead of 8 percent. Which project should the firm take now?

Project S: (1 − 1.16−2) / 0.16 = 1.605232

6,500 × 1.605232 = 10,434.01, so NPV = 434.01

Project L: 15,000 / 1.560896 = 9,609.87, so NPV = −390.13

Take S. The answer has flipped, and L is now worse than doing nothing at all, because its NPV is negative. At 16 percent you would reject L even if it were the only project on the table.

Nothing about either project changed. Only the rate changed. The two lines in the picture cross at about 9.93 percent, and 16 percent is on the far side of that crossing.
